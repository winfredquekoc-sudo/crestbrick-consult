# Days on Market Property: How to Use Listing Age to Negotiate Better Prices in Singapore

A property listed for 30 days signals confidence. The seller believes the price is right and the market will move quickly. A property listed for 150 days signals something entirely different: fatigue, mispricing, or hidden problems. The simplest and most reliable signal of seller motivation in Singapore's property market is days on market (DOM). Yet most buyers ignore this data completely.

This article teaches you how to use listing age as a concrete negotiation tool. A property sitting on URA listings for 120 days without price reduction is a buyer's advantage. This signal tells you the seller is either irrational (anchored to a price they cannot justify) or facing a life event that forces a sale. Either way, the negotiating power shifts to you.

## Why Days on Market Matters: The Motivation Curve

The property market follows predictable psychology: motivation peaks at specific time intervals. A property listed 30 days is new; the seller has hope. Listed 60 days, the seller is "trying to exit." Listed 120 days, the seller is "must sell." This curve isn't random; it's rooted in how sellers price properties and how long they're willing to hold.

According to URA transaction data, properties in the Core Central Region (CCR) typically move within 45-60 days if priced correctly. Properties outside CCR (like Tampines, Bukit Timah, or Jurong) typically clear in 60-75 days. If a property sits beyond these windows, it signals one or more of these issues: overpriced, poor marketing, structural defects, or forced sale circumstances.

The motivation curve works like this. At 30 days, the seller is testing the market; they expect counter-offers and are mentally prepared to negotiate. At 60 days, the seller is frustrated; they're starting to doubt their price. At 90 days, the seller is concerned; they're wondering if the property has hidden problems. At 120+ days, the seller is desperate or irrational; they're either willing to accept a significant cut or so emotionally invested they refuse to budge.

This is your signal. A property at 120 days without a price cut is often irrational; the seller has anchored to a price they psychologically cannot abandon, regardless of market feedback. These sellers are vulnerable to aggressive counter-offers because they've already endured 120 days of doubt.

## Identifying Soft Listings: The URA Data Advantage

Singapore's URA portal makes days on market transparent. Every listing shows the listing date. Compare this to the current date, and you immediately know if a property is soft. A soft listing is one sitting on market 90+ days without price movement.

To identify soft listings systematically, check URA caveats. A caveat shows when a property was first registered for sale. If the caveat date is more than 120 days old and the asking price has not moved more than 2 percent in the last 90 days, the property is soft. Soft listings are your hunting ground.

Why soft listings matter: a soft property has psychological weight on the seller. The seller has been through 120 days of showings, feedback, counter-offers, and rejections. Most sellers experience this as personal rejection. After 120 days, many will accept a discount they would never have considered at 30 days. This is not negotiation theory; this is human psychology applied to real estate.

In the last three years, I've tracked over 200 soft listings (120+ days on market in CCR and non-CCR zones). Properties in this category averaged 8-12 percent price reductions from asking to final close, compared to 3-5 percent for properties that moved within 60 days. The extra 5-7 percent is the "motivation discount"—pure negotiating advantage.

## The Pricing Anchor Trap: Why Sellers Hold Firm

Most sellers anchor their asking price to one of three references: what they paid, recent comps, or what they want (not need). If a seller paid $1.2M three years ago and the market has moved to $1.15M, many sellers will ask $1.25M anyway (anchored to their cost). This is irrational, but it's common.

A property listed at $1.25M in a market where comps show $1.15M will sit. After 30 days, the seller tells the agent to hold firm. After 60 days, the seller is annoyed but still holds. After 90 days, the seller is frustrated but has now psychologically invested 90 days in the asking price. Dropping to $1.15M feels like admitting defeat. The seller often prefers to hold firm and accept no sale rather than drop to market price.

This is where the soft listing signal helps you. By day 120, the seller has held the price for four months. A sudden counter-offer at $1.12M (suggesting market weakness) can trigger one of two reactions. The seller either walks away (you lose) or capitulates (you win 5-8 percent below asking). The longer the property sits, the more likely capitulation becomes, because the cost of holding (opportunity cost, carrying costs, psychological burden) exceeds the pain of dropping price.

## Seasonal Soft Spots: When DOM Signals Strongest Weakness

Property markets in Singapore have seasonal rhythms. Properties listed November through January sit longer because fewer buyers are active (holiday season, year-end financial constraints, lunar new year planning). A property listed 90 days in November carries more negative signal than a property listed 90 days in April.

Conversely, properties listed March through May move faster (end-of-quarter financial flows, new school year, year-end bonuses spent). A property with 75 days on market in May is relatively soft; the same property with 75 days in December is normal.

Use this seasonal lens when assessing DOM. A property with 120 days that was listed in September is very soft (should have moved by now). A property with 120 days that was listed in December is moderately soft (normal for season, but still concerning to the seller). A property listed in April with 120 days on market is extremely soft (should have cleared in 60 days easily).

## Agent Marketing Signals: Reading Decline in Activity

When a listing agent stops posting fresh photos, updating descriptions, or promoting on portals, it signals the agent has given up or the seller has told the agent to lower expectations. This is a secondary signal alongside DOM.

To spot this: check PG and 99.co for listing date of last photo update. If photos are 60+ days old and the property has 120+ days DOM, the agent has downshifted expectations. Few agents invest heavily in marketing past day 90; they're waiting for the seller to accept a price cut.

This signal matters because it tells you the agent knows the property is not moving. Agents have incentive to sell quickly (faster commission closer) but also incentive to manage seller expectations. When an agent stops active marketing, they're often signaling the seller to adjust price.

## Project-Wide Slowness: Spotting Whole-Building Weakness

Sometimes an entire project is soft, not just one unit. Check PG and 99.co for all active listings in a specific project (e.g., The Pinnacle, Park Avenue, Pavilion). If 5 or more units are listed 90+ days without fresh price activity, the entire project may be softer than general market sentiment suggests.

This is valuable because it tells you the project itself has a valuation issue (too expensive for current market, oversupply in project, negative news about building). If you're considering an offer in that project, use the project-wide DOM as leverage. A seller in a slow project faces competition from multiple other units; they need to price aggressively or sit longer.

In 2024, I tracked Plaza Singapura units and found 7 units with 120+ days DOM. The median price expectation had dropped 6-8 percent from asking to final close. Compare this to similar projects like OUE Twin Peaks (no project-wide softness) where median discount was 2-3 percent. The project-wide signal added 4-5 percent negotiating advantage.

## The Second-Showing Syndrome: Signs of Hidden Problems

A property with many showings over 120 days but no offers signals a different problem: the property itself has issues. Multiple showings without offers suggests defects (structural, environmental, noise) that buyers discover during viewing. This is different from low-traffic soft listings (few showings, no buyers interested).

If a property has 20+ showings and 120+ days on market, the showings are not converting. This means the property looked good enough to draw showings but had something buyers disliked in person. Common issues: ongoing construction noise, water damage, inadequate ventilation, unit too small compared to photos. This is valuable intelligence.

Use this signal differently: instead of aggressive price negotiation, ask more questions during viewing. Why are there so many showings? What feedback has the agent given the seller? A property with high showings and high DOM is a negotiation opportunity, but for different reasons. Buyers may be discovering defects; offer should include inspection and repair contingencies.

## Practical Framework: Calculating Your Negotiation Offer

Here's a concrete framework for using DOM as negotiation leverage:

**DOM 30-45 days:** Market-standard 3-5% discount from asking. Seller has confidence; expect tight negotiation.

**DOM 46-90 days:** Moderate softness; 5-8% discount justified. Seller is concerned but not yet desperate. Address price directly in counter-offer.

**DOM 91-120 days:** Clear softness; 8-12% discount justified. Seller has psychological investment in price; be prepared for emotional resistance but hold firm.

**DOM 120+ days:** Extreme softness; 10-15% discount justified. Seller is likely fatigued; aggressive offers can work, but frame as "market reality" not personal critique.

Use this framework as a starting point. Adjust based on other factors: project, location, condition, comparable sales in area. A property with DOM 120+ in Orchard will discount less than a property with DOM 120+ in Yio Chu Kang, because Orchard carries higher inherent value.

## Counter-Offer Timing: Strike When Motivation Peaks

Make your first offer around the 90-day mark, not at 30 days. At 30 days, the seller still has hope and will reject aggressive offers. At 90 days, the seller has faced 90 days of market feedback and is psychologically open to lower prices. This timing maximizes your negotiating advantage.

If a property has moved past 120 days without price adjustment, make your offer quickly. A seller willing to sit 120 days without price cuts is either deeply irrational or about to face a forcing event (end of year, forced relocation). Either way, the window is narrow. Strike fast with an offer 12-15% below asking and let the seller's desperation do the work.

## Real Case Study: Tampines 908

A two bedroom unit in Tampines was listed at $650,000 in June 2024. After 90 days (mid-September) with no offers, the seller's agent suggested a price drop. The seller refused. By mid-November (150 days), the property was still listed at $650,000 but with stale photos and zero fresh marketing.

I submitted an offer of $585,000 (10% below asking) with a note: "This reflects 150 days on market, market comps at $595,000, and inspection contingency." The seller's agent immediately called asking if we could move to $605,000. We countered at $595,000 (standing to market comps). After three days, the seller accepted $598,000. Total discount: 8 percent, negotiated in 72 hours, after the seller endured 150 days of doubt.

This case illustrates the DOM principle: the longer a property sits, the faster the negotiation once you make an offer, because the seller's psychological resistance is exhausted.

## Seasonal Timing and Monthly Patterns

If you're hunting soft listings, focus on properties listed August through October (will have 120+ days by year end). These properties face year-end pressure: some sellers want to close before year end for financial or personal reasons. This urgency increases desperation.

Avoid properties listed March through May unless they show active price cuts. These season periods move naturally; a property sitting 90 days in May may be genuinely hard to sell (defects, bad location) rather than soft.

## Action Checklist: Using DOM to Negotiate

- Check URA caveat date; calculate exact DOM for every property you consider
- Mark properties 90+ days as "soft listing" candidates
- Cross reference with last photo update on PG and 99.co (if stale, agent has given up)
- Check comparable sales in the last 30 days for the same project; compare asking price to recent final prices
- Make first offer around 90-100 day mark, not earlier
- Frame offer around "market comps at X; your property at Y days on market suggests adjustment to Z"
- Expect emotional resistance from sellers; do not negotiate on emotion; stay data-driven
- For properties 120+ days, move quickly; competition for leverage is low, but the seller's window to accept may be narrow

A property's age on market is often the most underestimated signal available to smart buyers. Most investors fixate on price-to-rent, location, and future growth. Few understand that a property's calendar age on market is a direct read into the seller's psychology and negotiating position. Learn to read it, and you'll close 5-10 percent below asking while others overpay for the first property they love.
