# Co-Ownership Structures: Joint Tenancy vs Tenancy in Common

When two or more people own a property, the legal ownership structure matters for inheritance, financing, and liability. Singapore recognizes two main structures: Joint Tenancy and Tenancy in Common.

## Joint Tenancy (Most Common for Couples)

**How it works:**
- Both owners have equal, undivided rights to the property
- If one owner dies, their share automatically passes to the other owner (right of survivorship)
- No probate needed; ownership transitions immediately

**Example:**
- Husband and wife buy HDB as joint tenants
- Husband dies; wife automatically becomes sole owner
- No will needed for this property

**Advantages:**
- Automatic inheritance (clean estate settlement)
- Simplified for married couples

**Disadvantages:**
- Both owners are equally liable for the mortgage
- If one owner wants to sell their stake, must sell the entire property (can't split)

## Tenancy in Common (Independent Stakes)

**How it works:**
- Each owner holds a distinct, divisible share (e.g., 50%/50% or 40%/60%)
- If one owner dies, their share goes through their will or to their estate
- Each owner can independently sell or mortgage their share (though buyer usually buys whole property)

**Example:**
- Father and adult child buy condo as tenants in common (50%/50%)
- Father dies; his 50% goes to his estate (via will), not automatically to child
- Child's 50% remains child's; father's 50% is distributed per will

**Advantages:**
- Clear, independent ownership shares
- Each owner can specify what happens to their share (via will)
- Useful for non-spouse co-ownership (business partners, friends, parents + children)

**Disadvantages:**
- Probate required on deceased owner's share (delays estate settlement)
- More complex for financing (lender must clarify which owner's share secures the loan)

## Financing Implications

**Joint Tenancy:**
- Mortgage is typically on both owners' names jointly
- Both are equally liable
- If one dies, survivor inherits the property AND the mortgage liability (bank requires proof of death, reassesses survivor's ability to pay)

**Tenancy in Common:**
- Mortgage can be structured to be held by one owner (or both)
- Lender scrutinizes the arrangement closely; they want security on the whole property, not a partial share
- More complex loan terms

## Tax Implications (Singapore)

**Stamp Duty on Purchase:**
- No difference between joint tenancy and tenancy in common; both pay the same

**Inheritance Tax (Debts):**
- Joint tenancy: Deceased's debts must be paid from their CPF or personal assets, not from the inherited property
- Tenancy in common: Deceased's share is subject to estate duties; creditors can claim against the share

**Rental Income (if renting out):**
- Joint tenancy: Income split equally (taxed per owner's share)
- Tenancy in common: Income splits per ownership percentage (e.g., 40%/60%)

## Estate Planning Impact

**Joint Tenancy (automatic inheritance):**
- Simpler if you want everything to pass to your spouse
- Does NOT follow your will (bypasses probate)
- Risk: If you change your mind about your spouse (e.g., contemplating divorce), automatic inheritance lock-in is problematic

**Tenancy in Common (testamentary inheritance):**
- Your will controls where your share goes (to spouse, children, charities, etc.)
- Allows flexibility (e.g., 50% to spouse, 50% to children from previous marriage)
- Requires probate; longer estate settlement

## Switching Between Structures

**Can you convert joint tenancy to tenancy in common?**

**Yes, but it requires:**
1. Written application to land registry (ACRA in Singapore)
2. Both owners' consent
3. Stamp duty and legal fees ($500–1,500)
4. Takes 2–4 weeks

**Why switch?**
- Relationship breakdown (easier to split ownership clearly)
- Estate planning (want independent inheritance rights)
- Business partnership (clearly define financial stakes)

## Common Scenario: Married Couple

**Most couples default to joint tenancy:**
- Simplicity; automatic inheritance to surviving spouse
- Reduced probate headaches

**But consider tenancy in common if:**
- You have children from previous marriages (want them to inherit their "slice")
- You want to leave property to charities or trusts (not automatic to spouse)
- Estate planning is complex

## Checklist: Which Structure Should You Choose?

- [ ] Are you married? (Yes/No) → Joint tenancy typical
- [ ] Do you have children from previous marriages? (Yes/No) → Tenancy in common may be better
- [ ] Are you buying with a business partner or friend? (Yes/No) → Tenancy in common (independent stakes)
- [ ] Do you want automatic inheritance to the co-owner? (Yes/No) → Joint tenancy
- [ ] Will you be independent sellers in future? (Yes/No) → Tenancy in common allows individual sale of stake
- [ ] Do you have a complex estate plan? (Yes/No) → Tenancy in common gives more flexibility
