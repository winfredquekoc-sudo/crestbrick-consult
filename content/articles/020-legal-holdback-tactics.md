# Property Closing Protection: Escrow, Contingencies, and Legal Holds for Safe Transactions

Closing a property is the most vulnerable moment in a transaction. Funds have been wired, keys are about to change hands, and the deal is about to become irreversible. At this final moment, a seller can disclose a defect previously hidden, a critical repair can fail, or a title issue can emerge. These late surprises are rare but devastating when they occur.

This article teaches the legal protections available at closing: escrow holds, contingencies, title checks, and final walkthroughs. These are your safety mechanisms to ensure you don't cross the line to completion with unknowns lurking.

## The Escrow Mechanism: Holding Funds Until Conditions Are Met

Escrow is the standard mechanism in Singapore property transactions. When you pay the purchase price to the seller, the funds don't go directly to the seller. Instead, the funds are held in escrow (typically by the seller's lawyer) until all conditions of the sale have been met.

Standard conditions for escrow release: (1) All outstanding mortgages on the property are paid off from the sale proceeds; (2) All title issues are resolved (encumbrances cleared, liens satisfied); (3) All agreed repairs have been completed and verified; (4) Final walkthrough shows no new damage or missing agreed fixtures; (5) All title documents are clear and transferred to buyer.

Only when these conditions are satisfied does the escrow release the funds to the seller.

The typical escrow amount is 5-10 percent of the purchase price, held by the seller's lawyer. If you're buying a $1M property, roughly $50,000-$100,000 is held in escrow pending closing conditions.

For properties with known defects (known roof leak, known electrical issues), increase the escrow hold to 10-15 percent or specifically designate portions of escrow for repair verification. Example: If the seller agreed to repair a $30,000 roof leak before completion, hold $35,000-$40,000 in escrow specifically for roof repair verification by a structural engineer.

## Contingencies: Your Legal Exit Routes

An offer should include multiple contingencies, which give you the right to renegotiate or withdraw under specific conditions. The major contingencies in Singapore property transactions:

**Inspection Contingency:** "This offer is conditional upon satisfactory structural and systems inspection within 14 days of offer acceptance. If inspection reveals defects requiring repairs exceeding $X, Buyer may renegotiate or withdraw without penalty."

**Financing Contingency:** "This offer is conditional upon Buyer obtaining mortgage financing at X percent interest rate or lower. If financing is not available, Buyer may withdraw without penalty."

**Title Contingency:** "This offer is conditional upon satisfactory review of the property title and all encumbrances. If title issues exist that cannot be resolved, Buyer may withdraw without penalty."

**Renovation/Repair Contingency:** "If Buyer requests repairs or renovations before completion, Seller must complete or escrow funds for completion. Buyer retains the right to verify completion by licensed contractor before funds are released."

Not all offers include all contingencies; most include inspection and financing. A solid offer includes at least three of the four. These contingencies are your legal exits if major issues emerge post-offer.

## Title Insurance and Title Checks: Protecting Against Hidden Encumbrances

Singapore property titles are generally clear and registered with the Singapore Land Authority (SLA). However, hidden issues can exist: undisclosed liens from past owners, easements (rights of way affecting the property), or caveats (claims against the property).

A title check is essential. Your lawyer will perform a title search through SLA records to identify any encumbrances, easements, or caveats registered against the property. This search takes 3-5 working days and costs $200-$400.

Title insurance is optional but recommended. Title insurance protects you against hidden title defects discovered after closing (e.g., a lien from a previous owner's debt, an easement allowing a third party access through your property, or a caveat claiming ownership by someone other than the seller). Title insurance costs roughly 0.5-1 percent of purchase price (Singapore is relatively low risk, so premiums are modest).

If the title search reveals issues, your lawyer can request the seller's lawyer to clear them before completion (pay off liens, remove caveats, provide legal documentation regarding easements). If issues cannot be cleared, your title contingency allows you to renegotiate or withdraw.

Real case: A property had a caveat registered against it from an ex-spouse claiming a property interest. The caveat was dormant (old dispute from 15 years prior) but still registered. The seller's lawyer had to contact the ex-spouse to obtain a release of the caveat before completion could proceed. This took an additional week and delayed closing. Without the title contingency, the buyer could have been forced to complete even with this uncertainty.

## The Final Walkthrough: 48 Hours Before Completion

A final walkthrough is essential. This typically occurs 24-48 hours before completion and serves two purposes: (1) verify that the property condition remains as negotiated (no new damage); (2) confirm that all agreed-upon fixtures and fittings are still present.

During the final walkthrough, check:
- **Structural integrity:** Look for new cracks, water damage, or subsidence
- **Fixtures and fittings:** Verify all agreed-upon items are present (air conditioning units, kitchen appliances, light fittings)
- **Systems functionality:** Test electricity (lights, outlets), water (pressure, hot water), drainage, aircon
- **Damage or neglect:** Look for new damage, missing paint, or signs of the seller stripping the property
- **Utilities:** Confirm utilities are live and functioning

If new damage is discovered (new water stain, cracked tiles, missing fixtures), you have the right to withhold completion pending repair or compensation. Document everything with photos. Do not proceed to completion if there are unresolved issues.

In one case, a buyer discovered the seller had removed custom kitchen cabinets during the final walkthrough (the cabinets were agreed to be included). The buyer refused completion until the cabinets were reinstalled. The seller scrambled to reinstall them, and completion proceeded. Without the final walkthrough and the buyer's willingness to halt, the buyer would have completed without the cabinets and had no recourse.

## Contingencies Framework: Inspection, Financing, Title

**Inspection Contingency in Action:** You make an offer with an inspection contingency. After inspection, defects are discovered (water damage, structural cracks, electrical hazards totaling $40,000 in repairs). You have the right to: (1) Renegotiate price downward by $30,000-$40,000; (2) Require the seller to complete repairs before completion and verify completion through a licensed contractor; or (3) Withdraw from the offer without penalty and recover your earnest money deposit.

**Financing Contingency in Action:** You make an offer with a financing contingency requiring a mortgage at 3.5 percent interest. During underwriting, the bank offers only 4.2 percent interest (interest rates have risen, your credit score didn't improve as expected). The higher rate is not acceptable. You can withdraw from the offer without penalty because financing was not available at the contracted rate.

**Title Contingency in Action:** Title search reveals the property has an easement (a neighbor has the right to use part of your driveway). This easement is decades old but affects your use of the property. You can renegotiate the price to account for the easement, require the seller to obtain a release from the neighbor (expensive and risky), or withdraw if the issue cannot be resolved.

These contingencies are essential protections. Never waive them, and never agree to close without resolving contingency conditions.

## Repair Verification: The Contractor Certification Step

If the seller agreed to complete repairs before completion (roof leak, electrical work, plumbing), do not accept verbal confirmation. Require written certification from a licensed contractor. The contractor should provide: (1) A detailed scope of work completed; (2) Before/after photos; (3) Warranty or guarantee on the work; (4) Confirmation the work meets applicable building codes.

Hold escrow funds ($35,000-$50,000) until the contractor's certification is provided and verified by your engineer or a third-party inspector. Do not release funds based on the seller's word.

In one transaction, the seller claimed a $25,000 electrical repair had been completed. The buyer accepted the seller's word and completed. Post-completion, the buyer discovered the repair was incomplete (only 50 percent of the circuits had been addressed). The buyer had no recourse; the seller had already received all funds. The electrical repair cost an additional $15,000 to complete.

Lesson: always verify repairs with a contractor before completion.

## Utilities and Systems Testing: The Checklist Walkthrough

Before completion, systematically test every system:
- **Electrical:** Test every light switch, every outlet, verify no overloading
- **Water:** Check water pressure at sinks and showers; verify hot water works (allow 1-2 minutes for water to heat); check for leaks under sinks
- **Drainage:** Flush toilets, run water in sinks for 30 seconds to verify drainage works
- **Air conditioning:** Run the aircon unit; verify cool air flows; check for leaks
- **Gas:** If the property has gas, verify gas supply is connected and working
- **Internet:** Check if internet lines are functional (if important to you)
- **Fire safety:** Verify smoke detectors are present and functional

If any system fails, do not complete. Withhold completion and require the seller to repair before funds are released.

In a townhouse transaction, the buyer discovered during the final walkthrough that the septic tank system (critical for a landed property) was malfunctioning. The seller had not disclosed this. The buyer refused completion, withheld funds, and required the seller to replace the septic tank ($8,000 expense) before completion. The seller paid, and completion proceeded.

## Legal Checks: Ensuring the Seller Has Clear Selling Rights

Before completion, your lawyer should verify the seller has the legal right to sell the property. This sounds obvious, but complications can arise:
- **Ownership:** Is the seller the registered owner (check with SLA)?
- **Marital status:** If the property is owned by a married couple, are both signing the sale? If one spouse is missing, there could be complications.
- **Court orders:** Is the property subject to any court orders (e.g., from a divorce, bankruptcy, or debt collection)?
- **Power of attorney:** If the seller is using a power of attorney to execute the sale, is the POA valid and properly documented?

Your lawyer handles these checks, but be aware of them. If complications exist, completion can be delayed or jeopardized.

One case involved a property owned by an elderly property owner. The seller's child was managing the transaction using a power of attorney. However, the power of attorney had lapsed; it was never renewed. The lawyer discovered this during the title check, and the transaction had to be delayed while a new power of attorney was executed. This delayed completion by three weeks.

## Hold-Back Amounts: Calibrating Escrow to Risk

The amount held in escrow should match the level of risk. For a clean property with no known defects, 5 percent escrow is standard. For a property with known issues that the seller agreed to repair, increase escrow to 10-15 percent or allocate specific escrow portions to repair verification.

Example: A property has a known roof leak that the seller agreed to repair. Hold $50,000 in escrow designated for roof repair verification. Do not release this portion until a structural engineer certifies the roof repair is complete and watertight. Once certified, release the funds.

Another example: A property has structural cracks that the seller agreed to assess and repair if necessary. Hold $75,000 in escrow for structural repairs. Require the seller to obtain a structural engineering report post-completion and complete any recommended repairs within 90 days. Hold the escrow until repairs are verified.

## The Contingency Waiver Trap: Never Waive Too Early

Some sellers push buyers to waive contingencies early (before inspection, before financing is approved) to "show commitment" and make the offer "attractive." Never do this. Contingencies are your legal protections. Waiving them early removes your exit routes.

Some buyers waive the inspection contingency to compete in a multi-offer situation ("we'll waive inspection to show we're serious"). This is dangerous. If you skip inspection and later discover $40,000 in defects, you're locked in with no recourse. The 2-3 percent discount you thought you got by looking "serious" is more than erased by hidden repairs.

Keep contingencies in place until all conditions are resolved. Only waive them at the very end, when you've verified everything and are ready to close.

## Completion Checklist: The Final Safety Steps

Before you wire funds and complete:

- **Title search complete:** No liens, encumbrances, or caveats outstanding (or all resolved)
- **Financing approved:** Mortgage offer letter in hand, no conditions outstanding
- **Inspection complete:** All defects identified; renegotiations or repairs finalized
- **Final walkthrough done:** No new damage; all fixtures in place; all systems functional
- **Repairs verified:** Contractor certifications received for any pre-completion repairs
- **Escrow documented:** Escrow amount, conditions for release, and holdback amounts clearly specified
- **All contingencies resolved:** No outstanding inspection, financing, or title contingencies
- **Lawyer confirmation:** Your lawyer confirms all conditions met, title is clear, no legal issues outstanding
- **Utilities verified:** Electricity, water, gas, drainage all tested and working

Only after all items are checked should you authorize the wire transfer of funds and proceed to completion.

## Real Case: The Catch at Completion

A property in Clementi was purchased for $850,000. During the final walkthrough (24 hours before completion), the buyer discovered the built-in wardrobes had been removed (the wardrobes were included in the sale agreement). The seller claimed the wardrobes belonged to the previous tenant and had to be removed.

The buyer's lawyer immediately contacted the seller's lawyer: "The wardrobes are specifically listed as included fixtures. They must be reinstalled or the buyer will withhold completion and escrow funds." The seller scrambled to find replacement wardrobes (sourcing cost: $12,000, installation: $2,000). The seller covered the cost rather than lose the sale.

This buyer's preparation and final walkthrough saved them $14,000.
