# Premium Issue #03 — The lease decay trap most agents won't show you

**Tier:** Crestbrick Premium ($29/mo)
**Length:** ~1,780 words
**Single topic:** How Bala's Table actually values leasehold property, and why owners discover the trap at exit, not at entry

---

A client called me last December. He had bought a 99-year leasehold private condo in District 14 in 2008 — call it a 2,200 sqft duplex. He paid $1.05m. He was now 64, retiring, and wanted to sell to fund his daughter's overseas education and his own downsizing.

The property had 83 years of lease left. He assumed, reasonably, that since CCR and RCR resale prices had roughly doubled since 2008, his unit was worth around $2m.

The first valuation came back at $1.42m.

The second valuation came back at $1.38m.

He thought the valuers were wrong. They weren't. He had walked into the lease decay trap, and like most owners, he discovered it at the worst possible moment — at exit, with a hard deadline.

This issue is about the math behind that valuation, why it is structural, and what you should be doing today if you own — or are about to buy — leasehold property in Singapore.

---

## What Bala's Table actually is

Bala's Table is the leasehold-to-freehold valuation factor used by SLA, IRAS, and effectively every chief valuer in Singapore. It tells you what fraction of freehold value a leasehold property of any remaining tenure is worth. The table was published in 1998 and has not been formally revised, though courts and valuers reference updated rules of thumb. The original numbers — and their structural shape — are still the reference.

The shape of the table is not linear. It is a curve. That is the entire trap.

Here is the table, simplified for the tenures that matter most:

| Remaining lease (years) | % of freehold value (Bala's factor) |
|---|---|
| 99 | 96% |
| 90 | 94% |
| 80 | 92% |
| 70 | 89% |
| 60 | 85% |
| 50 | 80% |
| 40 | 73% |
| 30 | 60% |
| 20 | 43% |
| 10 | 22% |

Two things to notice.

**First — between 99 and 80 years remaining, the decay is gentle.** You lose roughly 4% of freehold-equivalent value over 19 years. Most owners buy in this zone and sit comfortably for a decade or two without seeing any obvious damage.

**Second — between 60 and 40 years remaining, the decay accelerates sharply.** You lose 12 percentage points over 20 years, then another 13 over the next 10. From 40 to 30, you lose 13 points in a decade. From 30 to 20, you lose 17 points.

The curve is convex. The pain compounds. And — critically — the pain is invisible to the owner because the absolute price the unit transacts at can still be flat or even rising in nominal terms while the lease-adjusted value is falling.

This is the trap.

## A real example — 60-year leasehold vs freehold, same district, same size

Take two units in District 15, both 1,300 sqft, both in similar condition, both fronting the same East Coast corridor. One is freehold (FH), the other has 60 years of lease remaining (60-LH) — meaning it was originally a 99-year that was bought 39 years ago, or a 60-year tenure issued by SLA.

Recent transactions, indicative of the District 15 corridor in 2025:

| Property | Tenure | Area | Transacted PSF | Total price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Freehold reference unit | FH | 1,300 sqft | $2,100 | $2,730,000 |
| 60-year leasehold unit | 60-LH | 1,300 sqft | $1,785 | $2,320,500 |

The 60-LH trades at 85% of freehold PSF. That matches Bala's Table almost exactly.

Now run the math forward 20 years. The leasehold owner who buys today and holds for two decades sees their lease drop from 60 to 40 years. The Bala factor drops from 85% to 73%. *In real terms relative to freehold, the unit has lost 14% of its value just from lease decay — before any market movement.*

If the market is flat over those 20 years, the freehold unit holds nominal value. The leasehold unit drops 14%. If the market rises 50%, the freehold rises 50%; the leasehold rises 28% (50% × 73/85). If the market falls 20%, the freehold falls 20%; the leasehold falls 31%.

Lease decay is not a tax. It is a structural drag on every leasehold asset, every year, accelerating over time.

## Why most owners discover the trap at sale

Three reasons, and all three are about how the trap is hidden during the holding period.

**1. The recent transaction prints look fine for a long time.**
A 70-year leasehold in a hot district can transact at higher nominal prices than its 80-year self did five years earlier — because the market rose more than the lease decayed. Owners see the print, feel reassured, and don't notice the relative decay against freehold comps.

**2. CPF withdrawal limits do the warning, but quietly.**
CPF imposes restrictions on using CPF funds for properties with leases that won't cover the youngest buyer to age 95. The first time many owners learn about lease decay is when their buyer's mortgage broker tells them the buyer can only use limited CPF — which forces a price cut. By that point, the seller has already mentally priced the unit at the wrong number.

**3. Bank financing tightens silently.**
Once a property's remaining lease falls below 60 years, banks reduce LTV, shorten tenure, and some refuse to finance at all. This shrinks the buyer pool. Demand thins. The unit may need to be discounted 10–15% just to find a buyer with cash.

The owner who bought a "99-year" condo in 2008 with 99 years of lease, and tries to sell it in 2032 with 75 years left, is not selling a 99-year asset. They are selling a 75-year asset into a buyer pool that is comparing it to fresher leasehold and freehold stock — and that pool has shrunk.

My client in the first paragraph? His 83-year unit was being valued at the right Bala factor. He hadn't done anything wrong. He had simply assumed time was free.

## The two scenarios where leasehold makes sense, and the one where it doesn't

I am not anti-leasehold. I have helped clients buy leasehold property profitably. But the rules are tight.

**Leasehold makes sense if:**

1. **The hold period is short (≤7 years) and the lease-decay drag is small.** A 99-year unit held from years 99 to 92 loses 2% of freehold-equivalent value. The market move usually swamps that. This is the playbook for upgraders who plan to recycle.
2. **The discount to freehold at entry is steeper than Bala's Table implies.** If a 60-year leasehold trades at 70% of freehold PSF in a district where Bala says it should be 85%, the market is already pricing in the decay plus a margin. You may be getting the asset on sale.

**Leasehold rarely makes sense if:**

3. **The hold period is long (>15 years) and the lease starts below 80 years.** This is where the convex part of the curve eats you. If you are 50 years old buying a 70-year lease "for retirement income," you are buying a 50-year asset that will be worth 80% of freehold-equivalent at year 1 and 60% at year 21, when you most want to sell or pass it on.

## Action — how to evaluate a leasehold property today

Five steps. Run all five before you sign anything.

1. **Look up the original tenure and remaining lease via SLA's INLIS portal.** Don't trust the marketing brochure. Get the official record.
2. **Pull the Bala factor at the current remaining lease, and at the projected remaining lease at your intended exit.** Compute the percentage of freehold value lost purely to decay over your hold period.
3. **Find three freehold comps in the same district, same size, similar condition.** Compute the freehold PSF benchmark.
4. **Compute the implied freehold value of the leasehold unit:** transacted PSF ÷ Bala factor at current lease. If that implied freehold value is *higher* than the freehold benchmark, the leasehold is overpriced. If it is meaningfully lower, you may be looking at a real discount.
5. **Stress-test the exit.** Project the Bala factor at year of sale. Run the freehold benchmark forward at 0%, +30%, and −20% market scenarios. The leasehold's nominal exit price in each scenario tells you whether you are actually making money or just holding a depreciating asset that the market is hiding for now.

If you own a leasehold property today with fewer than 70 years remaining and you are over 55, run this exercise this month. The trap closes faster than you think.

## What I would do

If I had $2.5m of investment capital today and the choice between a 60-year leasehold at $1,785 PSF and a freehold at $2,100 PSF in the same corridor, I would pay the freehold premium. The 15% upfront discount on the leasehold is fully consumed by the next 20 years of decay, and the asymmetry on exit is materially worse. The freehold doesn't make me rich. It just doesn't bleed.

For owner-occupation, leasehold is fine — you are buying shelter, not yield. For investment, the math has to work harder than most agents will tell you. Bala's Table doesn't lie. It just waits.
