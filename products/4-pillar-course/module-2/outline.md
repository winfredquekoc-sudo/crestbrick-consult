# Module 2 — Pillar 1: Affordability

**4-Pillar Investor Course**
**Module length:** ~60 min across 5 lessons
**Pre-requisite:** Module 1 complete (Decision-Mode self-score recorded, decision quiz passed)
**Pillar:** Pillar 1 — Affordability
**Module deliverable:** Personal Affordability Ceiling spreadsheet + Pillar 1 score (1–10) + max safe quantum

**Learning objective:** By the end of Module 2, the student can compute their *true* affordability ceiling — distinct from the bank's TDSR-driven approval — and stress-test it against rate moves, income shocks, and life events on a 10-year horizon.

---

## Lesson 2.1 — The Bank Number Is the Wrong Number

**Runtime:** 10 min

The bank tells you what you can borrow. Pillar 1 asks what you can *sustain*. Those two numbers are almost never the same — and the gap between them is where households quietly break at year 7. This lesson covers exactly what TDSR (Total Debt Servicing Ratio) and MSR (Mortgage Servicing Ratio, for HDB and EC) actually compute: the variable income haircut, the medical-stress-test rate (currently 4.0% for residential), how IPP / variable bonus is treated, and why the bank's number is structurally optimistic. We finish with the Crestbrick rule of thumb: take the bank's max approval and multiply by 0.75. That's your starting Pillar 1 ceiling. Modules 2.2–2.5 will tell you whether to push it up or down from there.

---

## Lesson 2.2 — The Hidden Affordability Layers

**Runtime:** 12 min

Banks check debt servicing. They don't check the *rest* of your household balance sheet. This lesson covers the four hidden layers that the bank ignores but Pillar 1 demands: **(1) opportunity cost** — what your CPF and cash could have earned elsewhere, and how that changes the real cost of capital; **(2) liquidity** — how much you have left after down payment + BSD + renovation + furniture, and the 6-month emergency reserve floor; **(3) dependents** — child education trajectory (international school is a $40K–$60K/year line item that rarely appears in affordability talks), and aging parents; **(4) lifestyle commitments** — car, insurance, recurring expenses you don't realise are fixed. We build the household Real Affordability Layer, which is what gets stress-tested in Lessons 2.3 and 2.4.

---

## Lesson 2.3 — Stress-Testing for +200bps and Income Shock

**Runtime:** 13 min

The 2022–2023 rate cycle moved Singapore mortgage rates from ~1.3% to ~4.2% in roughly 18 months. Households that had passed the bank's stress test still felt it, because the bank's stress test rate (~4%) was effectively *the new actual rate*. Pillar 1 stress-testing goes further: we model **+200bps from today's rate** as the baseline scenario, **+300bps** as the bear case, and overlay **one-income-gone for 12 months** (parental leave, retrenchment, sabbatical, medical). The lesson walks through exactly how to layer these stresses on top of the Affordability Ceiling spreadsheet, and what "passes" vs "borderline" vs "fails" looks like numerically. Output: a green/yellow/red Pillar 1 self-mark.

---

## Lesson 2.4 — Year 7: When Affordability Becomes Sustainability

**Runtime:** 12 min

Most affordability conversations stop at year 1. Pillar 1 cares about year 7 — because that's where the second-order failures show up. Year 7 is roughly when: the kids hit primary school costs, the car loan from year 2 finishes but a replacement gets bought, parents need supplementary medical capital, and the original loan's principal-heavy reduction phase begins. This lesson maps the typical Singaporean household's year-by-year cost evolution from year 1 to year 10, identifies the three "compression points" (years 3, 7, and 9 in the typical curve), and shows how to size your Pillar 1 ceiling so that the *worst* year still clears — not just year 1. Affordability is a 10-year exam, not a closing-day exam.

---

## Lesson 2.5 — The Stretch-and-Pray Trap

**Runtime:** 10 min

The Pillar 1 failure mode I see most often: a household stretches to maximum bank approval because "we'll grow into it." Sometimes they do. Often they don't, and the result is a forced sale at a bad time, or a holding period extended by exhaustion rather than design. This lesson catalogs the five most common over-extension patterns: **(1) the bonus-dependent buy** (40%+ of debt service relies on variable income); **(2) the dual-income lock-in** (no margin if one parent steps back); **(3) the short-tenure stretch** (10-year loan term to qualify, that crushes monthly cash flow); **(4) the ignored second-order** (school fees, parent care, started after signing); **(5) the rate-anchor mistake** (treating the floating rate at signing as the long-run rate). Each comes with the diagnostic question and the fix. We close Module 2 with the Affordability Ceiling spreadsheet handover and the Pillar 1 score gating into Module 3.
