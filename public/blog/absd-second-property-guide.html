ABSD Second Property Singapore 2026: Rates, Decoupling, and the All-In Cost

Current ABSD rates explained, who pays what, the decoupling option, timing strategies, and a worked example showing every dollar you need to budget for.

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick · Updated April 2026 · ~900 words

Singapore's Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty is the single largest variable cost in any property purchase beyond the first. For a Singapore Citizen buying a second property at S$1.5 million, ABSD adds S$300,000 to the transaction, on top of the purchase price, BSD, legal fees, and any renovation budget. That is not a rounding error. It is a six-figure cash outlay that has to be sourced in cash (not CPF) within the 14-day payment window after signing the OTP.

This article covers what you need to know before you sign anything.

Current ABSD Rates (2026)

ABSD was last revised in April 2023. As of 2026, the rates are:

Buyer Profile1st Property2nd Property3rd+ Property
Singapore Citizen (SC)0%20%30%
Singapore Permanent Resident (SPR)5%30%35%
Foreigner (non-EFW nationality)60%60%60%
Foreign nationals (select FTA countries)15%15%15%
Entities (companies, trusts)65%65%65%

Joint purchases apply the highest applicable rate. If a Singapore Citizen buys jointly with a foreigner (their spouse, for example), the 60% foreigner rate applies to the entire purchase unless a remission scheme applies. This is one of the most common and costly surprises in mixed-nationality couples' first property purchase.

Who Actually Pays ABSD

ABSD applies to each property purchase based on the highest count across all buyers listed on the title. Property count is assessed individually and as a combined total where joint ownership is involved.

This means:

If a Singapore Citizen husband owns one property in his name, and they want to buy a second property in both names, the husband counts as buying his second property (20% ABSD). The wife, if she owns nothing, counts as her first (0%). The joint purchase triggers the higher rate, so ABSD applies to the full purchase price at 20%.

If the same wife buys it in her name alone, she is buying her first property, and no ABSD is triggered. This is the basis of the decoupling strategy.

The Decoupling Option

Decoupling means restructuring ownership of your current property so that one spouse exits the title, freeing up the other to be a "first-time buyer" for the next purchase.

How it works in practice: a couple co-owns Property A. Spouse A transfers their share to Spouse B (or vice versa) via a sale or gift. After the transfer, Spouse A is no longer on the title of any property. Spouse A can now buy Property B as a first-time buyer with 0% ABSD.

The catch: the transfer of the share is itself a stamp-duty event. BSD applies on the share transferred at normal rates. Legal and valuation fees apply. And if there is an outstanding mortgage, the bank's consent is required, which may trigger a repricing or refinancing event.

Timing Strategies

Three timing approaches apply to couples or individuals facing the second-property ABSD:

1. Sell first, buy second (remission route). If an SC couple sells their existing property and buys a replacement together, they qualify for an ABSD remission, provided the replacement purchase and the original disposal both complete within specific timeframes. The ABSD is paid upfront and refunded on application. This is not a waiver; the cash must be available at signing.

2. Decouple before buying. Transfer your current property's co-ownership to one name, then the freed-up spouse buys the next property solo. Works best when the current property is at or near full equity (low outstanding loan) and the BSD on the transfer is less than the ABSD on the new purchase.

3. Accept and plan around ABSD. Sometimes the math says: pay the ABSD, the investment case still holds. A rental-yield property generating 4% gross on S$1.5M over 10 years produces S$600,000 in gross rent. S$300,000 in ABSD is recovered in 5 years of rent at full occupancy. If the capital appreciation case is strong, ABSD is a sunk cost that does not change the long-term return materially.

Worked Example: All-In Cost for a Second Property Purchase

Scenario: Singapore Citizen couple buying a second private condo at S$1,500,000.

Total all-in cost (low)~S$1,873,100
Total all-in cost (high)~S$1,924,900

Note: ABSD must be paid in cash within 14 days of the date the OTP is exercised. It cannot be paid from CPF. The S$300,000 must be liquid before you sign.

BSD is calculated on a tiered basis: 1% on first S$180k, 2% on next S$180k, 3% on next S$640k, 4% on next S$500k, and 5% on amounts above S$1.5M. At S$1.5M, BSD is S$39,600.

What the Decoupling Math Looks Like

If the couple in the above example currently co-owns a condo at S$1.2M with a S$400k outstanding loan, and Spouse A transfers their 50% share (valued at S$600k) to Spouse B:

BSD on the S$600k transfer is approximately S$14,400. Legal fees S$3,000. Total restructuring cost: ~S$17,400. If that restructuring allows Spouse A to buy the S$1.5M second property with 0% ABSD instead of 20%, the saving is S$300,000. The restructuring saves S$282,600 net.

The decoupling math almost always works in the buyer's favour when the second property is S$1M or above, and the current property has sufficient equity to complete the transfer cleanly. The cases where it does not work are when the outstanding loan is large, the bank repricing cost is high, or the time pressure makes the process impossible to complete before the OTP deadline.
