When to Upgrade from HDB to Condo in Singapore: A Timeline That Actually Works

By Winfred Quek, CEA R073319H, Crestbrick  |  April 2026  |  1,200 words

Most HDB owners know they want to upgrade eventually. But "eventually" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Upgrade too early and you pay unnecessary stamp duties, leave CPF accrued interest to compound, or find yourself short on cash for the private downpayment. Upgrade too late and you've watched years of capital appreciation pass by.

The question is not just "can I afford it?" The question is: what is the right window, based on your flat's MOP status, CPF position, outstanding loan, and the private market you're entering?

This guide walks through every variable in the upgrade timeline decision, so you can make it with numbers, not instinct.

The Non-Negotiable Starting Point: MOP

The Minimum Occupation Period (MOP) is the baseline. You cannot sell your HDB flat to fund a private upgrade until MOP is complete. For most flats, that is five years from the key collection date (not the purchase date).

PLH (Prime Location Housing) flats have a ten-year MOP. If you bought at Kallang Whampoa, Rochor, or other prime estates under the PLH scheme, your timeline is materially longer and requires a different planning approach.

Your MOP completion date is the earliest possible upgrade date. Everything else layers on top of that.

CPF Accrued Interest: The Silent Cost That Grows Every Year

When you used CPF OA to buy your HDB, you did not spend that money outright. You borrowed it from your own retirement account. At the time of sale, CPF requires you to refund both the principal amount withdrawn and the interest it would have earned if left in the account (currently 2.5% per annum, compounded).

This accrued interest does not reduce your war chest directly. The money goes back into your CPF OA, where you can use it again for the next downpayment. But it does reduce the cash you walk away with, and that matters for meeting the 5% cash-only requirement on a private purchase.

Example: You withdrew $120,000 CPF OA to buy your flat in 2019. By 2026 (7 years), accrued interest at 2.5% compounds to roughly $21,400. Total CPF refund at sale: $141,400. That $21,400 returns to your OA and is reusable. But your cash proceeds are lower by that amount.

The practical implication: the longer you hold, the higher the CPF refund. For planning purposes, model your CPF refund at the exact year you intend to sell.

The War Chest Calculation: What You Actually Have

Your "war chest" is the total capital available for a private property purchase after selling your HDB. It has two components: cash proceeds and CPF OA restored.

The formula:

War chest = Sale price - Agent commission (1%) - Legal fees (~$5,500) - Outstanding HDB loan

The war chest is constant regardless of when you sell (holding longer does not increase it unless the flat appreciates). What changes with time is how the war chest splits between cash and CPF.

ABSD: The Cost of Owning Two Properties at Once

If you plan to buy private before selling your HDB (buy-first approach), you will own two properties simultaneously. For Singapore Citizens, the second property attracts 20% Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty (ABSD).

On a $1.4 million condo, that is $280,000 in ABSD. You can apply for an ABSD remission after selling the HDB, but only if you sell within six months of the private purchase completion date. Miss that window and the $280,000 is gone.

The sell-first approach avoids ABSD entirely but requires you to rent in between (bridging period). For most families with children in school or settled in a neighbourhood, this disruption has a real cost. Factor it in when comparing approaches.

The Right Window: When the Numbers Line Up

There is no universal "right time." But there is a framework for reading when your numbers are aligned:

MOP complete: The floor. Non-negotiable.

Outstanding loan below 30% of flat value: The lower your remaining loan, the higher your cash proceeds and the cleaner the financial picture for the new bank's TDSR assessment.

War chest covers total upfront for your target price point: Total upfront for a $1.4M condo (SC, first private) is roughly BSD ($39,600) + 25% downpayment ($350,000) + legal fees (~$3,500) = approximately $393,000. If your war chest covers this with room to spare, you have genuine optionality.

Combined income supports the monthly mortgage: A $1.4M condo with 75% LTV ($1.05M loan, 30 years, 1.6% rate) costs roughly $3,680 per month. Under TDSR at 55%, your combined gross monthly income needs to be at least $6,690 per month after existing debt obligations.

When all four conditions are met simultaneously, you are in the window. When one is missing, you know exactly which one to work on first.

One Timing Mistake That Costs Upgraders Dearly

Waiting for the "perfect" market moment. Private property in Singapore has not delivered a sustained entry opportunity in the post-2020 period, and most analysts do not expect one in the near term. The upgrader who waited for a 10-15% correction in 2022 and 2023 is now looking at prices that have moved further from their HDB proceeds.

The better question is not "is the market high?" but "does my financial position allow me to enter comfortably and hold for at least seven to ten years?" If yes, the entry timing matters less than people think. Singapore private property rewards long hold periods far more reliably than it rewards market timing.

The Summary

The HDB upgrade timeline has six key variables: MOP status, CPF accrued interest, outstanding loan balance, war chest size, ABSD exposure from overlapping ownership, and monthly income vs TDSR. None of these exist in isolation. The right upgrade window is when all six line up, not just one or two.
