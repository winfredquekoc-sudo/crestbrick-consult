ABSD Singapore 2026: Everything Foreigners and PRs Need to Know

By Winfred Quek, Crestbrick · CEA R073319H · Updated July 2026

What this article covers

  • Current ABSD rates for foreigners, PRs, and Singapore Citizens in 2026
  • The SC/foreigner married couple remission — conditions, limits, and the smarter structure
  • Payment deadline, what happens if you miss it, and how ABSD is calculated
  • When you can claim an ABSD refund and how to apply
  • What you should do before making any offer

The first thing most foreigners feel when they hear "60% stamp duty" is disbelief. Then they do the arithmetic on a S$2 million condo and feel something worse. Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty is the Singapore government's primary lever for managing foreign demand in the residential market, and it is deliberately punishing. Understanding exactly how it works, who it hits, and where the narrow carve-outs exist is not optional if you are a foreigner or PR considering a Singapore purchase.

This is not a general overview. It covers the current 2026 rates, the mechanics, the remission rules, the refund pathways, and the ownership structures worth knowing. Read it before you walk into any showroom or sign any OTP.

What is ABSD and who has to pay it?

Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty is a tax levied by IRAS (the Inland Revenue Authority of Singapore) on top of the standard Buyer's Stamp Duty whenever you purchase residential property in Singapore. BSD applies to everyone. ABSD is the additional layer that targets second-time buyers, PRs, and foreigners.

The categories that trigger ABSD are straightforward:

  • Singapore Citizens buying a second or subsequent residential property
  • Singapore Permanent Residents buying any residential property
  • Foreigners buying any residential property
  • Entities (companies, trusts) buying any residential property

ABSD is computed on the full purchase price or the market value, whichever is higher. This catches people who try to understate the contract price — IRAS will get an independent valuation and stamp you on whichever number is larger. On a S$1.5 million purchase with 60% ABSD, that is S$900,000 in stamp duty alone before BSD. You pay both.

The duty is payable on the property as a whole, not on your share of it. If you and a co-purchaser each own 50%, ABSD is still computed on 100% of the purchase price and charged at the rate applicable to the buyer with the highest applicable rate. This point matters enormously for mixed-citizenship couples, which I cover in detail below.

What are the current ABSD rates in 2026 for foreigners, PRs, and Singapore Citizens?

The rates have not changed since April 2023, when the government doubled the foreigner rate from 30% to 60% as part of a cooling package. As of July 2026, these are the rates in force:

Buyer profile1st property2nd property3rd and beyond
Singapore Citizen (SC)0%20%30%
Singapore PR (first purchase)5%30%30%
Foreigner60%60%60%
Entity (company, trust)65%65%65%

A few things are worth making explicit. For foreigners, there is no first-timer relief. The 60% applies from the first purchase and stays at 60% regardless of how many properties you own. For PRs, the first purchase attracts a much lower 5%, but the second jumps sharply to 30%. And for Singapore Citizens, the first property carries zero ABSD, but the second brings in 20% and the third 30%.

To put the numbers in context: on a S$1.8 million condo purchase by a foreigner, ABSD alone is S$1,080,000. On top of BSD of approximately S$44,600, the total stamp duty bill before any other costs exceeds S$1.1 million. That is the financial reality of foreign residential ownership in Singapore.

Is there any way for foreigners to avoid or reduce ABSD?

There is one meaningful carve-out: nationals from countries that have a Free Trade Agreement with Singapore covering stamp duty treatment. The FTA nationals who qualify for SC-equivalent treatment on their first residential property only are:

  • United States nationals
  • Nationals of Switzerland, Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway (EFTA states)
  • Nationals of EU member states (under the EU-Singapore FTA)

If you fall into one of these categories and are buying your first Singapore residential property, you pay zero ABSD on that first purchase, just as a Singapore Citizen would. A second purchase would be treated at the SC second-property rate of 20%, not 60%.

This benefit applies to individuals only, not entities. If you are an American buying through a company or trust, you do not get the FTA concession. The company pays the entity rate of 65%.

Outside of FTA status, there is no legal mechanism that reduces the 60% rate for foreigners. The "99-to-1" tenancy-in-common structures that briefly circulated as ABSD mitigation strategies attracted IRAS scrutiny from 2023 onward. IRAS has the authority to treat such arrangements as tax avoidance and impose penalties and interest on the full ABSD. Any structure that is primarily designed to reduce stamp duty rather than serve a legitimate commercial or personal purpose carries real legal and financial risk. If someone is recommending an "ABSD avoidance structure" to you, get a second opinion from a Singapore property lawyer before signing anything.

What about married couples with one SC and one foreigner?

This is where most of the confusion sits, and where the most consequential decisions are made. Let me be precise about how this works.

When a married couple buys together as joint purchasers, ABSD is computed at the rate of the buyer with the higher applicable rate. So if an SC and a foreigner buy jointly, the entire purchase price is stamped at 60% — the foreigner rate. You do not average the rates or split them by ownership share. The full purchase price attracts the highest applicable rate.

There is, however, an ABSD remission available for SC/foreigner married couples when buying their first residential property together as their matrimonial home. The conditions are strict:

  • Both spouses must be listed as joint purchasers on the OTP and Sale and Purchase Agreement
  • Neither spouse may own another residential property at the time the remission is granted
  • The property must be the couple's matrimonial home (they must jointly occupy it)
  • The couple must remain legally married
  • The remission application must be submitted to IRAS within 6 months of the purchase

If all conditions are met, the couple can receive a remission of the ABSD that would otherwise apply. In effect, for this first and only property, the purchase is treated on par with an SC first-time purchase — zero ABSD.

But here is the strategic reality. This remission covers only the first property. The moment the couple wants to buy a second property, the foreign spouse's presence on the ownership structure re-introduces the 60% exposure. And if circumstances change — the marriage ends, one spouse buys something in their own name — the remission conditions become a complication.

The cleaner structure in most cases is for the SC spouse to purchase as the sole owner. No joint purchase, no remission application needed, no ongoing compliance conditions. The SC buys as a first-time buyer: zero ABSD. If and when they buy a second property later, they pay 20% as an SC second-property buyer, not 60%. The foreign spouse has no ownership in the Singapore property, which has its own implications for estate planning and security of tenure, but the stamp duty position is unambiguous and future-proof.

Which route is right depends on your specific situation: income, CPF eligibility, loan eligibility, and your longer-term property plans. These are the conversations worth having before you put pen to OTP.

How does ABSD work for PRs buying their first versus second property?

PRs sit in the middle ground — materially better treatment than foreigners, but meaningfully worse than Singapore Citizens.

On the first residential property, a PR pays 5% ABSD. This is a real cost, not trivial, but at least it is not prohibitive. On a S$1.2 million resale condo, that is S$60,000 in ABSD on top of BSD. Painful but manageable for most buyers with a proper financial plan.

The second property is where the jump is severe: 30% ABSD. On the same S$1.2 million condo, that is S$360,000. Many PRs who buy a second property do so after upgrading, and they face a meaningful decision about whether to sell their first property before or after purchasing the second.

One pathway PRs sometimes consider is decoupling, where a PR and SC spouse structure ownership to free up one spouse's "first buyer" status for the next purchase. This is a legitimate strategy but involves its own costs — Buyer's Stamp Duty on the inter-spouse transfer, legal fees, potential CPF refund implications, and loan restructuring. The numbers have to work before committing. The ABSD Decoupling Calculator on this site gives a quick read on whether the structure makes financial sense for your property values.

For PRs considering their first purchase, the strategic question is usually not whether to buy but what to buy. Getting the first property right matters more when you are building toward a second purchase later, because the 30% ABSD on that second property means you need the first property to have performed well enough to absorb that cost and still come out ahead. Yield, capital appreciation trajectory, and exit liquidity all matter more when your next transaction carries a 30% tax.

When do I pay ABSD, and what happens if I miss the deadline?

ABSD must be paid within 14 days of signing the Option to Purchase or the Sale and Purchase Agreement, whichever comes earlier. In practice, this means you need your financing, your cash, and your stamp duty funds ready before you sign anything — not after.

Missing the 14-day deadline triggers late payment penalties from IRAS. The penalty is 5% of the unpaid ABSD per annum, pro-rated daily, plus interest at 8% per annum on any unpaid amount beyond 6 months. IRAS does not send reminder notices. The clock runs from the moment you sign.

A common mistake I see with foreign buyers is underestimating the upfront cash required. BSD and ABSD together are payable at the same time. On a S$2 million property, if you are a foreigner, you are looking at approximately S$1,244,600 in total stamp duties (S$44,600 BSD plus S$1,200,000 ABSD at 60%) due within 14 days. That is not a sum you can scramble to arrange after the fact.

For PR first-time buyers the arithmetic is more manageable but still demands planning. On a S$1.2 million purchase, BSD is approximately S$29,600 and ABSD is S$60,000, totalling around S$89,600 due within two weeks of signing. Factor this into your liquidity plan before you start viewing.

Can I get an ABSD refund if my circumstances change?

There are two established refund pathways worth knowing:

PR to SC conversion. If you purchased a residential property as a PR, paid the 5% first-property ABSD, and subsequently obtained Singapore Citizenship, you can apply to IRAS for a refund of the ABSD paid on that first property. The conditions: you must have held it as your only residential property at the time you obtained SC status, and you must apply within 6 months of obtaining citizenship. This is not automatic — you have to file the application and provide the documentation.

SC/foreigner married couple remission. As covered above, the joint-purchase remission must be applied for within 6 months of purchase. If you paid ABSD upfront and subsequently meet all the conditions, IRAS will process the refund. The upfront payment is unavoidable even if you intend to claim the remission — you pay first, claim later.

There is no refund pathway for foreigners who later sell their Singapore property. Once you buy and pay ABSD, that cost is sunk regardless of how long you hold or what happens to the property's value. This is one reason why the ABSD regime functions as an effective deterrent — it front-loads the full tax cost with no exit-side relief.

The practical implication for investors is clear: at 60%, the property must appreciate by more than 60% just to break even on the ABSD alone, before BSD, loan costs, and agent commissions. The investment case for foreigners in Singapore residential property depends almost entirely on strategic necessity (a family base, a long-term lifestyle decision, a hedge against currency or political risk in their home country) rather than pure return. That is a legitimate reason to buy, but it should be entered with clear eyes on the math.

What should I do before making an offer?

The sequence matters. Here is what I walk through with every foreign or PR client before they view a single unit:

First, establish your ABSD profile. Your citizenship status, your spouse's citizenship if relevant, your current property ownership across all jurisdictions, and whether you hold any beneficial ownership through entities. ABSD is assessed on what IRAS can see and verify, and Singapore has information-sharing agreements with multiple countries. Be accurate.

Second, run the total cost of acquisition. BSD plus ABSD plus legal fees plus agent commission. For foreigners at 60%, this fundamentally changes what "affordable" means. A S$1.5 million property is not a S$1.5 million outlay — it is closer to S$2.5 million when stamp duties are included. Your financing plan needs to account for this.

Third, check FTA eligibility if you are from a qualifying country. If you are American, Swiss, Norwegian, Icelandic, from Liechtenstein, or from an EU member state, verify with a Singapore property lawyer that your specific situation qualifies before relying on the zero-ABSD assumption for a first purchase. The FTA benefit requires proper documentation at the point of purchase.

Fourth, if you are married with mixed citizenship, model both structures. Joint purchase with remission versus SC-only ownership. The right answer depends on your income split, CPF eligibility, loan eligibility, and what you plan to do with the Singapore property in the medium term. This is a 30-minute conversation that can save or cost you six figures, so have it before the OTP deadline.

Fifth, confirm your financing pre-approval. Singapore banks require foreign income documentation, and some are selective about lending to non-residents or non-PR applicants. Know your maximum loan quantum, your TDSR position, and your cash requirement for stamp duties before you put in any offer. There is no cooling-off period once the OTP is signed and the stamp duty clock starts.
