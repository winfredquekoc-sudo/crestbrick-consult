Seven things to check before you buy an investment property in Singapore

Quick answer: Seven checks decide most Singapore investment property outcomes: entry price measured against comparable transactions rather than the asking price, total holding cost and whether the position survives a bad year, financing headroom under a 55 percent TDSR and a stress test rate floor, the tax drag of ABSD going in and SSD coming out, whether tenant demand is structural or cyclical, how quickly this specific unit sells in a soft market, and whether the remaining lease still finances cleanly for the person who has to buy it from you. Work through all seven before you commit, not after.

General education, not investment advice. Every figure below should be verified against your own numbers with IRAS, CPF Board, HDB and your bank before you rely on it.

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · Updated August 2026

Most people evaluating an investment property in Singapore assess one thing properly and assume the rest. What decides the outcome sits in the parts nobody looked at: the cash the position eats while empty, the stamp duty on both ends that sets a minimum holding period, and the shrinking pool of buyers who can finance the unit when you want out.

What follows is a working checklist of seven things worth assessing before committing. Each can independently sink a purchase that looks fine on every other measure, so passing six of seven is not a pass. None of this is a recommendation to buy anything: property can and does fall in value, and leverage makes a bad purchase considerably worse.

1. Entry price against comparable transactions

The number you fully control

In short: The asking price is a marketing position; the transacted price of similar units is evidence. Your entry price is the one variable you fully control.

Anchor your offer to the ask and you are negotiating a discount off someone else's number. A lodged caveat records what a real buyer paid with financing a real bank approved, which is the number your future buyer and valuer will look at.

How to check it

  • Pull caveats for the development itself first. Same project, unit type and bedroom count, last six to twelve months. Recency beats volume.
  • Normalise for what moves price per square foot inside one development. Floor, stack and facing, whether a bay window or air conditioner ledge sits inside the quoted area, renovated against original.
  • Widen only when you must, and write down the adjustment. If you cannot articulate it, you are guessing.
  • Line the transactions up by date. Mid range over twelve months but top of the last three is a different proposition.
  • Get the bank valuation before you commit. Any gap below your agreed price becomes cash out of pocket.
What a weak answer looks like"It is below the asking price." "Units here go for around this much." Also weak: comparing only against listed units, since unsold asks show what buyers refused to pay.
The common mistakeTreating a discount as a bargain. A unit marked down 8 percent from an ask that sat 15 percent above the transacted range is still expensive.

2. Total holding cost, and whether it survives a bad year

The test is the bad year

In short: Rent minus mortgage is not cash flow. Full holding cost includes interest, maintenance, property tax at the non owner occupier rate, insurance, letting fees, repairs and empty months.

Every position looks solvent under its best case. The question is what happens in a year where the unit sits empty for a stretch, a major item needs replacing and your rate resets upward at once. Two items get left out routinely: property tax at the higher non owner occupier rate, applied progressively to the annual value with bands that change, and the 2.5 percent your CPF Ordinary Account stops earning, with accrued interest compounding until it is refunded on sale.

How to check it

  • Build the full annual outgoing list, not the mortgage. Interest, principal, maintenance or conservancy charges, property tax, insurance, and an honest repair allowance.
  • Add the letting costs that recur with every tenancy. Commission on each new lease, reinstatement, and the gap between tenants.
  • Assume a real vacancy period, not zero, then check it still stands if that happens two years running.
  • Stress the interest rate upward. Bank packages have recently sat around 1.5 percent, purely as an illustration; test higher, since your loan outlives the package.
  • Check the reserves behind the building. Ask the MCST for the sinking fund balance and whether a special levy has been discussed. A thin fund on an ageing development is an unsent bill.
What a weak answer looks like"The rent covers the mortgage." That compares one inflow against one outflow and ignores five others. Also weak: a gross yield with no net calculation behind it.
The common mistakeBudgeting from the best month rather than the worst quarter. Vacancy and repairs arrive together, when a tenant leaves and the unit needs work before the next will take it.

3. Financing headroom and the cash CPF cannot cover

Approval is not headroom

In short: TDSR caps all debt at 55 percent of gross monthly income, assessed at a stress test rate floor above what you will pay. Your loan to value limit steps down once you carry an outstanding housing loan, and part of the down payment must be cash CPF cannot substitute for.

Approval tells you the bank will lend. Headroom tells you what you can still do afterwards. TDSR counts everything, the new mortgage plus car loan, personal loans, card minimums and guarantees, capped at 55 percent of gross monthly income; for an HDB flat or executive condominium the Mortgage Servicing Ratio applies at the same time, capping the housing loan alone at 30 percent. Banks then assess repayment at a medium term rate floor well above your package rate, so the loan you qualify for is smaller than the headline implies. Loan to value is the third: 75 percent on a first housing loan, stepping down materially once you carry an outstanding one, with the cash share of the down payment rising as that limit falls.

How to check it

  • Compute TDSR with every obligation in it, including debts you are guarantor on.
  • Ask for the stress test floor rate in writing, recompute the maximum loan at that rate, and treat that as your real number.
  • Confirm which loan to value tier applies to you before setting a target price. The 75 percent ceiling assumes a first housing loan and standard tenure and age conditions.
  • Separate the cash portion from the CPF portion. Two figures, and the cash must exist in a bank account today, not in a bonus you expect.
  • Check your income treatment. Variable, self employed and foreign sourced income are commonly discounted before TDSR is applied.
What a weak answer looks like"The bank approved me, so I can afford it." Approval is a ceiling, not a plan. Also weak: an affordability calculation done at the promotional rate.
The common mistakeBorrowing to the maximum the rules permit. A position at the edge of the cap has no room for a rate reset, a vacancy or a change of job, and those arrive together.

4. Transaction tax drag on entry and exit

Two taxes on capital, one at each end

In short: ABSD on entry depends on your profile: Singapore Citizens pay 0 percent on a first residential property, 20 percent on a second, 30 percent on a third or subsequent. Permanent Residents pay 5, 30 and 35 percent. Foreigners pay 60 percent, entities or trusts 65 percent. SSD is charged on exit if you sell too soon. Together they set a minimum holding period whether or not you meant to have one.

These behave differently from every other cost: levied on capital rather than income, payable in cash, and due to IRAS within 14 days rather than settled out of proceeds. For properties purchased on or after 4 July 2025, Seller's Stamp Duty runs on a four year window at 16 percent in year one, 12 percent in year two, 8 percent in year three and 4 percent in year four. Properties bought earlier keep the older three year schedule of 12, 8 and 4 percent, and which attaches to you depends on your purchase date, not when you sell. Anyone paying ABSD on entry and facing SSD on an early exit must recover both, plus legal fees, agent commission and everything in criterion two, before a sale returns anything. That is what sets the minimum sensible holding period.

How to check it

  • Establish your ABSD rate from your actual profile. Citizenship or residency of every buyer on the title, and the properties each already holds. Where buyers differ, the higher rate generally applies to the whole purchase.
  • Check whether any remission genuinely applies before you rely on it. Remission routes carry conditions and hard deadlines, and one you assume but do not qualify for is a large cash bill.
  • Date stamp your SSD schedule and diarise when the last tier falls away.
  • Compute the round trip, not the entry. ABSD, Buyer's Stamp Duty, legal fees both ends, agent commission on sale, and any SSD at your intended exit. That total is the hurdle a sale must clear before you see a cent.
What a weak answer looks like"I will hold long term anyway, so SSD does not matter." Intentions are not liquidity: relocation, divorce and illness have all forced sales inside an SSD window. Also weak: quoting an ABSD rate from memory.
The common mistakeTreating stamp duty as a closing cost rather than part of the entry price. It is capital rent will never recover.

5. Tenant demand that is structural, not cyclical

Who rents here, and for how long

In short: A tenant pool anchored to something permanent, a campus, a hospital cluster, a transport interchange, behaves very differently from one anchored to a single employer or a construction project. Name the tenant, then ask what would make them stop existing.

The two kinds of demand fail differently. Structural demand, generated by an institution that is not moving, degrades slowly if at all. Cyclical demand, generated by a project phase or a single large employer, can vanish inside one lease cycle. The other half is honesty about who your tenant is: a four bedroom unit and a studio in one development draw from different pools, and because turnover is expensive, a smaller pool with longer tenancies can beat a larger pool that churns.

How to check it

  • Write down the specific tenant, in a sentence. Not "professionals". A household working within a defined commute, at a specific budget. If you cannot write it, you do not know the pool.
  • Identify the anchor and test its permanence. An MRT interchange or a teaching hospital is not going anywhere. A single tenant office building can empty at lease end.
  • Check the competing supply your tenant would also consider, across every unit in the same commute and budget.
  • Look at the completion pipeline nearby. New completions release a block of competing units at once, and one landing when your first lease expires is directly relevant.
  • Verify asking rents from live listings and recent leases in the same development.
What a weak answer looks like"It is near an MRT station, so it will always rent." The entire competing supply in that catchment shares the same station. Also weak: "there is an expat crowd here" with no sense of what employs them.
The common mistakeReading a currently tight rental market as a structural fact. This question is about what is still true in five years, because your holding period outlasts any point in the cycle.

6. Exit liquidity for this specific unit

How fast it sells in a soft market

In short: Liquidity is a property of your unit, not the market. A large development with many similar units gives buyers alternatives and gives you competition; an unusual unit has a smaller buyer pool and can take far longer to move. Ask how this unit sells in a soft market, before you own it.

Every exit strategy assumes a buyer, and the moment you most need one is the moment fewest are around. Development size cuts both ways: a large one gives steady volume and a clear reference price, but several near identical units may list alongside yours, and the fastest sale belongs to whoever cuts price first. A boutique development reverses it, with less competition but thinner evidence and slower financing. Unusual units appeal strongly to a few buyers and not at all to the rest, which is fine when the market is warm and painful when it is not.

How to check it

  • Count the total units and the units of your type. The second number is your competition on the day you list.
  • Count comparable units listed right now, then check days on market for the ones sitting. Long sitting listings at your price point are the clearest read on how the exit behaves.
  • Look at transaction volume through the last genuinely soft stretch. A development that kept transacting when the market was quiet has demonstrated liquidity.
  • Ask what makes this unit harder to sell than its neighbours. Low floor facing a wall, above the refuse point, a bedroom that cannot take a bed.
  • Check who your future buyer would have to be, and whether they can finance it. This connects to criterion seven and to that buyer's ABSD profile.
What a weak answer looks like"Good units always sell." They do, at a price, eventually. Liquidity is measured in time and in the discount required, and an answer mentioning neither is no answer.
The common mistakeBuying the feature that makes a unit special without pricing the illiquidity with it. The quirk you paid a premium for is often what narrows the buyer pool when you want out.

7. Lease, lease decay and financeability for your future buyer

Your exit price is what your buyer can borrow

In short: Remaining lease is not just a number that shrinks. Once it no longer covers the youngest buyer to age 95, CPF usage is restricted and loan tenure shortens, shrinking the pool who can finance the unit at all. Lease decay is not linear: it accelerates where those constraints bite.

This is skipped most often, because on a long lease it appears to be nothing at all. It matters anyway, because you are not the last owner. The mechanism is the age 95 rule: for full use of CPF savings, the remaining lease must cover the youngest buyer using CPF to at least age 95. Where it does not, permitted CPF usage is pro rated, and where the lease is very short CPF cannot be used at all. Loan tenure is squeezed too, and higher instalments run straight back into the buyer's TDSR. Confirm the thresholds and the formula with CPF Board, HDB and the lender.

A younger buyer needs more lease to reach age 95 than an older one, so as a lease shortens the buyer pool skews older, and toward buyers with more cash and less need for leverage. That smaller, more price sensitive pool is the one you will sell into.

How to check it

  • Get the remaining lease from the official record, then compute what remains at your exit date rather than today.
  • Run the age 95 test for your likely future buyer, not yourself. Take a plausible buyer's age at your exit date and check whether the lease then remaining covers them to 95 with full CPF usage.
  • Check the effect on loan tenure at that point, and what the instalment does to that buyer's TDSR. This is where a lease constraint becomes a price constraint.
  • For an HDB flat, check the lease against every buyer in the household, because the youngest governs.
  • Do not price in an en bloc or redevelopment outcome. Those are possibilities, not plans.
What a weak answer looks like"There is still plenty of lease left." Plenty relative to what, and whose age? Also weak: assuming freehold removes the question. It removes the decay mechanism, but every other criterion still applies, and freehold carries a premium to be earned back.
The common mistakeMeasuring the lease against your own holding period rather than your buyer's financing. Whoever buys it from you has to finance the tail, and their bank and CPF rules set your ceiling.

The honest downside of leverage

In short: Leverage multiplies the outcome in both directions. On a 75 percent loan, a 10 percent fall in value wipes out roughly 40 percent of your equity. A large enough fall leaves you owing more than the property is worth, and the loan does not shrink because the market did.

The mortgage balance is fixed in dollars; the property value is not, and every dollar of a fall comes out of your equity first. Two Singapore specific consequences follow. CPF used in the purchase must be refunded on sale with accrued interest, ahead of any cash reaching you, so in a falling market you can sell, discharge the loan, refund CPF and walk away with little or nothing despite years of payments. And if a valuation falls materially, a lender may ask you to top up the shortfall in cash. There is also an opportunity cost rarely seen in any spreadsheet, since CPF deployed into a property stops earning the 2.5 percent Ordinary Account rate, against the 2.6 percent an HDB concessionary loan charges.

Property does not only go up. Singapore has had extended flat and falling stretches, and cooling measures have been introduced, tightened and adjusted repeatedly, in ways nobody predicted reliably. Nothing here forecasts a price, a yield or a return. The purpose of the seven checks is not to find a winner. It is to be sure you understand what you are taking on, and could survive being wrong.

Run the self check

If you cannot answer one with a specific number or fact, that is the criterion to go and work on. Vagueness on any line is usually where the problem hides.

  • 1. I can state the transacted price range for genuinely comparable units, with datesRecent caveats for the same unit type, adjusted for floor, stack and condition.
  • 2. I have a full annual holding cost figure, and I can fund a bad year in cashInterest, maintenance, property tax, insurance, letting costs, repairs and vacancy months, with a named funding source.
  • 3. I know my TDSR position at the stress test floor rate, not the package ratePlus what capacity remains, and cash separated from CPF.
  • 4. I have computed ABSD on entry and SSD at my realistic exit pointOn the correct schedule for my purchase date, with the round trip a sale must clear.
  • 5. I can describe my tenant in one specific sentence, and name what anchors themPlus the competing supply and pipeline that tenant would also look at.
  • 6. I know how many comparable units exist and how long they take to sell when the market is quietUnits of my type, current listings, days on market, volume in the last soft stretch.
  • 7. I have run the age 95 test for my likely future buyer at my intended exit dateRemaining lease at exit, that buyer's CPF usage and tenure, and the price they could pay.
  • 8. I have worked out what happens if I am wrongA fall in value, an extended vacancy and a rate reset together, and whether the position survives all three.

Passing seven of eight is not a pass. Each line is a way the position can fail on its own, and the weakest sets the outcome. If a purchase only works when you skip a criterion, that criterion is telling you something.
