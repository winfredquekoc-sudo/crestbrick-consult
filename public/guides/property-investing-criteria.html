Seven things to check before you buy an investment property in Singapore

Quick answer: Seven checks decide most Singapore investment property outcomes: entry price measured against comparable transactions rather than the asking price, total holding cost and whether the position survives a bad year, financing headroom under a 55 percent TDSR and a stress test rate floor, the tax drag of ABSD going in and SSD coming out, whether tenant demand is structural or cyclical, how quickly this specific unit sells in a soft market, and whether the remaining lease still finances cleanly for the person who has to buy it from you. Work through all seven before you commit, not after.

General education, not investment advice. Every figure below should be verified against your own numbers with IRAS, CPF Board, HDB and your bank before you rely on it.

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · Updated August 2026

Most people evaluating an investment property in Singapore assess one thing properly and assume the rest. They study the price, or they study the rent, and everything else gets a nod. The things that actually decide the outcome tend to sit in the parts nobody looked at: the cash the position eats while it is empty, the stamp duty on both ends that quietly sets a minimum holding period, and the shrinking pool of buyers who can still finance the unit when you want out.

What follows is a working checklist of seven things worth assessing before committing. They are deliberately non overlapping, and each can independently sink a purchase that looks fine on every other measure, which is why passing six of seven is not a pass. For each you get what it is, how to check it, what a weak answer sounds like, and the common mistake. A self check sits at the end. None of this is a recommendation to buy anything: property can and does fall in value, and leverage makes a bad purchase considerably worse, which is covered honestly in its own section below.

1. Entry price against comparable transactions

The only number in the whole exercise you fully control

In short: The asking price is a marketing position. The transacted price of genuinely similar units is evidence. Your entry price is the single variable you have real control over, and it is the one most buyers negotiate around rather than against.

An asking price tells you what a seller hopes for. Anchor your offer to it and you are negotiating a discount off someone else's number. A lodged caveat records what a real buyer paid with financing a real bank approved, and that is the number your future buyer and future valuer will look at.

How to check it

  • Pull caveats for the development itself first. Same project, same unit type, same bedroom count, last six to twelve months. Recency beats volume.
  • Normalise for what moves price per square foot inside one development. Floor level, stack and facing, whether a bay window or air conditioner ledge sits inside the quoted area, renovated against original.
  • Widen only when you must, and write down the adjustment. If you cannot articulate what you adjusted for, you are guessing.
  • Line the transactions up by date. Mid range against the last twelve months but top of the last three is a different proposition from mid range in both.
  • Get the bank valuation before you are committed. Banks lend against valuation, and any gap below your agreed price becomes cash out of your pocket.
What a weak answer looks like"It is below the asking price." "The agent says the seller is motivated." "Units here go for around this much." Also weak: comparing only against currently listed units, since a stack of unsold asks is evidence of what buyers have refused to pay.
The common mistakeTreating a discount as a bargain. A unit marked down 8 percent from an asking price that sat 15 percent above the transacted range is still expensive. Measure against the evidence, never against the ask.

2. Total holding cost, and whether it survives a bad year

The test is not the good year, it is the bad one

In short: Rent minus mortgage is not cash flow. The full holding cost includes interest, maintenance, property tax at the non owner occupier rate, insurance, agent fees on each new tenancy, repairs, and the months with no tenant in it at all. Model the bad year, not the brochure year.

Every position looks solvent under its best case. The question worth answering is what happens in a year where the unit sits empty for a stretch, a major item needs replacing, and your rate resets upward at once. Two Singapore specific items get left out routinely: property tax is charged at the higher non owner occupier rate once you are not living there, applied progressively to the annual value, with bands that change, so take current ones from IRAS. And anything funded from your CPF Ordinary Account stops earning 2.5 percent, with accrued interest compounding against you until it is refunded on sale.

How to check it

  • Build the full annual outgoing list, not the mortgage. Interest, principal, maintenance or conservancy charges, property tax at the non owner occupier rate, insurance, and an honest allowance for repairs.
  • Add the letting costs that recur with every tenancy. Agent commission on each new lease, reinstatement between tenants, and the gap between one tenant leaving and the next arriving.
  • Assume a real vacancy period, not zero. Pick empty months you can genuinely fund, then check the position still stands if it happens two years running.
  • Stress the interest rate upward. Bank packages have recently sat around 1.5 percent, purely as an illustration; test with a meaningfully higher number, because your loan will outlive the package.
  • Check the reserves behind the building. Ask the MCST for the sinking fund balance and whether a special levy has been discussed. A thin fund on an ageing development is a bill with your name on it, not yet sent.
  • Name the funding source for the bad year. Cash, not "I would sell something".
What a weak answer looks like"The rent covers the mortgage." That compares one inflow against one outflow and ignores five others. Also weak: a gross yield figure with no net calculation behind it, or assuming full occupancy because the last tenant renewed.
The common mistakeBudgeting from the best month rather than the worst quarter. Vacancy and repairs do not arrive evenly, they arrive together, usually when a tenant leaves and the unit needs work before the next one will take it.

3. Financing headroom and the cash CPF cannot cover

Approval is not the same as headroom

In short: TDSR caps all your debt obligations at 55 percent of gross monthly income, assessed at a stress test rate floor above what you will actually pay. Your loan to value limit steps down once you already carry an outstanding housing loan, and part of the down payment must be cash that CPF cannot substitute for.

Approval tells you the bank will lend. Headroom tells you what you can still do afterwards. TDSR counts everything, the new mortgage plus car loan, personal loans, credit card minimums and guarantees, capped at 55 percent of gross monthly income, and for an HDB flat or executive condominium the Mortgage Servicing Ratio applies at the same time, capping the housing loan alone at 30 percent. Banks then assess repayment at a medium term rate floor set well above your package rate, so the loan you qualify for is smaller than the headline rate implies. Loan to value is the third mechanic: 75 percent of the lower of price or valuation on a first housing loan, stepping down materially once you already carry an outstanding one. Part of the down payment must be cash that CPF cannot cover, and that cash share rises as the loan to value limit falls.

How to check it

  • Compute TDSR with every obligation in it, including debts you are guarantor on and any facility you have drawn.
  • Ask for the stress test floor rate in writing, recompute the maximum loan at that rate, and treat that as your real number.
  • Confirm which loan to value tier applies to you before you set a target price. The 75 percent ceiling assumes a first housing loan and standard tenure and age conditions.
  • Separate the cash portion from the CPF portion explicitly. Two figures, and the cash figure must exist in a bank account today, not in a bonus you expect.
  • Check your income treatment. Variable, self employed and foreign sourced income are commonly discounted before TDSR is computed.
  • Model what is left afterwards. Does your remaining capacity still permit the next thing you plan to do?
What a weak answer looks like"The bank approved me, so I can afford it." Approval is a ceiling, not a plan. Also weak: an affordability calculation done at the promotional rate, or one where CPF is quietly assumed to cover the whole down payment.
The common mistakeBorrowing to the maximum the rules permit. A position built at the edge of the cap has no room for a rate reset, a vacancy or a change of job, and those tend to arrive together.

4. Transaction tax drag on entry and exit

Two taxes, both on capital, one at each end

In short: ABSD is charged on entry and depends on your profile: Singapore Citizens pay 0 percent on a first residential property, 20 percent on a second and 30 percent on a third or subsequent. Permanent Residents pay 5, 30 and 35 percent. Foreigners pay 60 percent, and entities or trusts 65 percent. SSD is charged on exit if you sell too soon. Together they set a minimum holding period, whether or not you meant to have one.

These two behave differently from every other cost: levied on capital rather than income, payable in cash, and indifferent to whether the property performed. Stamp duty is due to IRAS within 14 days, so it is not settled later out of proceeds. For properties purchased on or after 4 July 2025, Seller's Stamp Duty runs on a four year window at 16 percent in year one, 12 percent in year two, 8 percent in year three and 4 percent in year four. Properties bought before that date keep the earlier three year schedule of 12, 8 and 4 percent, and which one attaches to you depends on your purchase date, not on when you sell.

Put the two together and the arithmetic gets uncomfortable. Anyone paying ABSD on entry and facing SSD on an early exit has to recover both, plus legal fees, agent commission and everything in criterion two, before a sale returns anything at all. That is what sets the minimum sensible holding period, and it is a consequence of the tax structure rather than a preference.

How to check it

  • Establish your ABSD rate from your actual profile. Citizenship or residency of every buyer on the title, and the residential properties each already holds. Where buyers differ, the higher rate generally applies to the whole purchase.
  • Check whether any remission genuinely applies before you rely on it. Remission routes carry conditions and hard deadlines, and one you assume but do not qualify for is a large cash bill with a short fuse.
  • Date stamp your SSD schedule, then diarise the exact date the last tier falls away.
  • Compute the round trip, not the entry. ABSD, Buyer's Stamp Duty, legal fees on both ends, agent commission on sale, and any SSD at your intended exit. That total is the hurdle a sale must clear before you see a cent.
  • Reconcile the hurdle against your actual timeline. If there is any realistic chance you need the money back inside the SSD window, the tax drag belongs in the decision now.
What a weak answer looks like"I will hold long term anyway, so SSD does not matter." Intentions are not liquidity. Relocation, divorce and illness have all forced sales inside an SSD window. Also weak: quoting an ABSD rate from memory, or assuming a remission applies without confirming conditions and deadlines in writing.
The common mistakeTreating stamp duty as a closing cost rather than part of the entry price. It is capital you will never recover through rent.

5. Tenant demand that is structural, not cyclical

Who rents here, and would they still be here in five years

In short: A tenant pool anchored to something permanent, a campus, a hospital cluster, a business district, a transport interchange, behaves very differently from one anchored to a single employer or a construction project. Name the tenant, then ask what would have to happen for that tenant to stop existing.

Rental demand is not a property of a district, it is a property of the people who need to be near something, and the two kinds fail differently. Structural demand, generated by an institution that is not moving, degrades slowly if at all. Cyclical demand, generated by a project phase or a single large employer, can vanish inside one lease cycle and takes your rent with it. The other half is honesty about who your tenant actually is: a four bedroom unit and a studio in the same development draw from entirely different pools, and because turnover is expensive, a smaller pool with longer tenancies can beat a larger pool that churns.

How to check it

  • Write down the specific tenant, in a sentence. Not "professionals". A two person household working within a defined commute, at a specific budget, for a specific reason. If you cannot write it, you do not know the pool.
  • Identify the anchor and test its permanence. An MRT interchange or a teaching hospital is not going anywhere. A single tenant office building can be vacated at the end of a lease.
  • Check the competing supply your tenant would also consider, across every unit within the same commute and budget, not just inside the development.
  • Look at the upcoming completion pipeline nearby. New completions release a block of competing units at once, and one landing around the time your first lease expires is directly relevant.
  • Check the unit against how that tenant lives. Layout efficiency, whether bedrooms take a bed and a wardrobe, whether a carpark lot is needed and available. Mismatches show up as longer vacancy, not lower rent.
  • Verify asking rents from live listings and recent leases in the same development, not from a yield figure quoted in a brochure or by a seller.
What a weak answer looks like"It is near an MRT station, so it will always rent." Proximity is one input, and the entire competing supply in that catchment shares it. Also weak: "there is an expat crowd here" with no sense of what employs them.
The common mistakeReading a currently tight rental market as a structural fact. Rental markets move with supply completions and hiring cycles. This question is deliberately about what is still true in five years, because your holding period is longer than any point in the cycle.

6. Exit liquidity for this specific unit

How fast it sells when nobody wants to buy

In short: Liquidity is not a property of the market, it is a property of your unit. A large development with many similar units gives buyers alternatives and gives you competition; a very unusual unit has a smaller pool of buyers and can take far longer to move. Ask how this specific unit sells in a soft market, before you own it.

Every exit strategy assumes a buyer, and the moment you are most likely to need one, when something has gone wrong and you need capital out, is the moment fewest are around. Development size cuts both ways. A large development produces steady transaction volume, giving valuers and buyers a clear reference price and making financing straightforward, but several near identical units may be listed alongside yours, and the fastest sale in that group belongs to whoever cuts price first. A boutique development reverses it: less competition, thinner transaction evidence, harder valuation, slower financing. Unusual units carry their own risk. A very large penthouse, a heavily customised layout, anything with a distinctive quirk, appeals strongly to a small number of buyers and not at all to the rest, which is fine when the market is warm and painful when it is not.

How to check it

  • Count the total units in the development and the units of your type. That second number is your competition on the day you list.
  • Count comparable units listed right now, then check days on market for the ones that have been sitting. Long sitting listings at your price point are the clearest read available on how the exit behaves.
  • Look at transaction volume through the last genuinely soft stretch. A development that kept transacting when the market was quiet has demonstrated liquidity. One that went silent demonstrated the opposite.
  • Ask what makes this unit harder to sell than its neighbours. Low floor facing a wall, above the refuse point, a bedroom that cannot take a standard bed, no carpark lot. Each narrows the buyer pool at exit even when invisible at entry.
  • Check who your future buyer would have to be, and whether they can finance it. This connects directly to criterion seven, and to the ABSD profile of the segment you would be selling into.
  • Put a realistic number of months on a soft market sale, then check whether you could fund the holding cost for that whole period without selling anything else.
What a weak answer looks like"Good units always sell." They do, at a price, eventually. Liquidity is measured in time and in the discount required, and an answer mentioning neither is not an answer. Also weak: pointing at how quickly units sold during a strong stretch, which tells you about the market, not the unit.
The common mistakeBuying the feature that makes a unit special without pricing the illiquidity that comes with it. The quirk you paid a premium for is often exactly what narrows the buyer pool when you want out.

7. Lease, lease decay and financeability for your future buyer

Your exit price is set by what your buyer can borrow

In short: Remaining lease is not just a number that shrinks. Once the lease no longer covers the youngest buyer to age 95, CPF usage is restricted and loan tenure shortens, which shrinks the pool of people who can finance the unit at all. Lease decay is not linear, and it accelerates precisely where those financing constraints bite.

This one is skipped most often, because on a long lease it appears to be nothing at all. It matters anyway, because you are not the last owner: whatever constrains your buyer's financing constrains your exit price. The mechanism is the age 95 rule. For full use of CPF savings toward a property, the remaining lease must cover the youngest buyer or owner using CPF to at least age 95. Where it does not, permitted CPF usage is reduced on a pro rated basis, and where the remaining lease is very short, CPF cannot be used at all. Loan tenure is squeezed by the lease too, and a shorter tenure means higher instalments, which runs straight back into the buyer's TDSR. Confirm the thresholds and the pro rating formula with CPF Board, HDB and the lender, because they turn on both the lease and the buyer's age.

Follow that forward. A younger buyer needs more remaining lease to reach age 95 than an older one, so as a lease shortens the buyer pool does not shrink evenly. It skews older, and toward buyers with more cash and less need for leverage. That is a structurally smaller and more price sensitive pool, and it is the pool you will be selling into.

How to check it

  • Get the remaining lease from the official record, not a listing description, then compute what remains at your intended exit date rather than today.
  • Run the age 95 test for your likely future buyer, not for yourself. Take the age of a plausible buyer at your exit date and check whether the lease then remaining still covers them to 95 with full CPF usage.
  • Check the effect on loan tenure at that point, and what the resulting instalment does to that buyer's TDSR. This is where a lease constraint converts into a price constraint.
  • Understand where on the decay curve you are buying. The value effect is mild for a long time and then steepens as financing constraints start to bind.
  • For an HDB flat, check the lease against every buyer in the household, because the youngest one governs.
  • Do not price in an en bloc or redevelopment outcome. Those are possibilities, not plans, and no owner controls them.
What a weak answer looks like"There is still plenty of lease left." Plenty relative to what, and to whose age? Also weak: assuming freehold or a 999 year tenure removes the question. It removes the decay mechanism, but every other criterion still applies, and freehold typically carries a price premium that has to be earned back.
The common mistakeMeasuring the lease against your own holding period rather than against your buyer's financing. You are not the one who has to finance the tail. The person who buys it from you is, and their bank and CPF rules set your ceiling.

The honest downside of leverage

In short: Leverage multiplies the outcome in both directions. On a 75 percent loan, a 10 percent fall in value wipes out roughly 40 percent of the equity you put in. A large enough fall leaves you owing more than the property is worth, and the loan does not shrink because the market did.

Borrowing is what makes property returns look large relative to the cash committed, and the same arithmetic works in reverse with no sympathy at all. The mortgage balance is fixed in dollars. The property value is not. When the second falls, every dollar of the fall comes out of your equity first, and a large enough fall leaves you owing more than the property is worth.

Two Singapore specific consequences follow. If you funded part of the purchase from CPF, that money must be refunded to your CPF account on sale together with accrued interest, ahead of any cash reaching you, so in a falling market it is entirely possible to sell, discharge the loan, refund CPF and walk away with little or no cash despite years of payments. And banks value the security behind their loans: if a valuation falls materially, a lender may ask you to top up the shortfall in cash, at the least convenient moment. There is also an opportunity cost that rarely appears in any spreadsheet, since CPF savings deployed into a property stop earning the 2.5 percent Ordinary Account rate, against the 2.6 percent an HDB concessionary loan charges on the other side of the ledger.

Property does not only go up. Singapore has had extended flat and falling stretches, and cooling measures have been introduced, tightened and adjusted repeatedly, in ways nobody predicted reliably in advance. Nothing on this page forecasts a price, a yield or a return, and any source that does should be treated with suspicion. The purpose of the seven checks is not to find a winner. It is to make sure you understand what you are taking on, and that you could survive being wrong.

Run the self check

Work through these before you commit. If you cannot answer one with a specific number or a specific fact, that is the criterion to go and do work on. Vagueness on any single line is usually where the problem is hiding.

  • 1. I can state the transacted price range for genuinely comparable units, with datesNot the asking price, and not the range of currently listed units. Recent caveats for the same unit type, adjusted for floor, stack and condition.
  • 2. I have a full annual holding cost figure, and I can fund a bad year in cashInterest, maintenance, property tax at the non owner occupier rate, insurance, letting costs, repairs and real vacancy months, with a named funding source.
  • 3. I know my TDSR position at the stress test floor rate, not the package rateIncluding what capacity remains afterwards, and with the cash portion of the down payment separated from the CPF portion.
  • 4. I have computed ABSD on entry and SSD at my realistic exit pointFrom my actual profile, with the correct SSD schedule for my purchase date, and I know the round trip cost a sale must clear.
  • 5. I can describe my tenant in one specific sentence, and name what anchors themAnd I have checked the competing supply and the completion pipeline that tenant would also be looking at.
  • 6. I know how many comparable units exist and how long they take to sell when the market is quietTotal units, units of my type, current listings, days on market, and transaction volume through the last soft stretch.
  • 7. I have run the age 95 test for my likely future buyer at my intended exit dateRemaining lease at exit, that buyer's CPF usage and loan tenure, and what it does to the price they can pay.
  • 8. I have worked out what happens if I am wrongA fall in value, an extended vacancy and a rate reset arriving together, and whether the position survives all three at once.

Passing seven of eight is not a pass. Each line represents a way the position can fail on its own, and the weakest one sets the outcome. If a purchase only works when you skip a criterion, that is the criterion telling you something.
