Your home loan got rejected after exercising the OTP: now what?

By Winfred Quek · 8 minute read · Published 13 July 2026

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 13 July 2026

Quick answer: Move fast on two fronts at once. Approach a mortgage broker or two to three other banks immediately to establish whether the rejection was bank specific or reflects a genuine affordability limit, and inform your conveyancing lawyer the same day so they can review your completion deadline and advise on real options. Most standard sale and purchase agreements do not build in an automatic financing contingency once the option is exercised, so a missed completion date because financing fell through is generally treated as a default, putting your exercised deposit at risk. This is different from defaulting on an existing mortgage after purchase, and the honest response is speed: the earlier you act, the more options remain open.

The message usually arrives at the worst possible time, a rejection notice from the bank days or weeks after you have already exercised the option and told everyone the purchase is happening. It is a genuinely stressful moment, and the instinct to freeze is understandable. It is also the wrong response. What actually determines your outcome here is how quickly and clearly you act in the days that follow, not the rejection itself.

Understand exactly where you stand first

Before doing anything else, get precise about your position. You have exercised the Option to Purchase, which means a binding sale and purchase agreement exists between you and the seller with a fixed completion date. This is a fundamentally different situation from a buyer who is still in the option period deciding whether to proceed, and it is also different from an owner who already completed the purchase and is now struggling to service an active mortgage, which I cover separately in my guide on what to do if you cannot pay your mortgage. Here, you do not yet own the property, and the immediate risk is not losing the roof over your head, it is losing your deposit and potentially the deal itself.

Step one, the same day: check whether this is a bank problem or an affordability problem

Banks apply their own credit assessment layered on top of the shared Total Debt Servicing Ratio framework, and a rejection from one lender does not automatically mean every lender will say no. Common reasons a specific bank rejects an application include how it treats variable or commission based income, existing credit facilities it weighs more conservatively, or a marginal TDSR case that another bank's internal policy might view differently. Contact a mortgage broker who works across multiple banks, or approach two or three lenders directly, as fast as you can. Their read on whether your file has a realistic path elsewhere is the single most useful piece of information you can get in the first 24 hours.

Step two, the same day: call your lawyer before you call anyone else about the deal

Your conveyancing lawyer needs to know immediately, not after you have exhausted every other option. They will review the exact completion deadline in your sale and purchase agreement, confirm how much runway genuinely remains, and advise you on what, if anything, is negotiable. This is not a step to delay out of embarrassment or hope that the problem resolves itself. The earlier your lawyer is looped in, the more room there usually is to manage the situation constructively, whether that means pursuing alternative financing on a realistic timeline or opening a conversation with the seller about the completion date.

There is usually no automatic financing contingency. Most standard sale and purchase agreements do not protect a buyer whose financing falls through after the option is exercised. The completion date is fixed, and failing to complete because your loan was rejected is generally treated the same as any other default, which can put your exercised deposit at risk. Confirm the specific wording of your own agreement with your lawyer rather than assuming a general protection exists.

Step three: weigh your realistic paths forward

Once you know whether alternative financing is genuinely available and how much time your completion deadline actually allows, you are choosing between a small number of paths. Securing financing from a different bank within the remaining timeline is the cleanest outcome if it is achievable. Asking the seller for a short extension to complete is possible but never guaranteed, since the seller is under no obligation to agree and any extension is a negotiated variation, sometimes with a fee attached, that should be properly documented through your lawyers rather than agreed informally. If neither path materialises in time, you are looking at the forfeiture consequences of a buyer default, which I unpack in detail in my guide on what happens if you forfeit your OTP deposit.

What tends to make the difference between a resolved case and a lost deposit

  • Speed. Every day spent hesitating is a day the completion clock does not pause for.
  • Being upfront with the seller's side early rather than going silent, which tends to harden the seller's position rather than soften it.
  • Having a mortgage broker in your corner who already knows which lenders are more flexible on the specific issue that caused the rejection.
  • Understanding the actual wording of your agreement instead of assuming general protections that may not exist in your specific case.

How to avoid this situation in the first place

The best fix for a loan rejection after exercising the option is never needing to write this article about your own case. Get an in principle approval or firm pre approval from a bank before you exercise, not just before you view. Understand your Total Debt Servicing Ratio position honestly, including how banks apply the stress test floor on top of your actual rate, before you commit. And if your income situation is even slightly unconventional, self employed, variable commission, or foreign sourced, get that conversation with a lender or broker done early, well before the option clock is running, so any issues surface while you still have the cheap option to walk away rather than after you have exercised.

Frequently asked questions

What should I do the same day my home loan gets rejected after exercising the OTP?

Move immediately on two fronts. First, contact your mortgage broker or approach at least two or three other banks to check whether the rejection reason, income documentation, TDSR calculation, or credit issue, is bank specific or a genuine affordability problem. Second, inform your conveyancing lawyer straight away so they can review the completion deadline in your sale and purchase agreement and advise on your options before time runs short. Waiting even a few days to act can materially narrow your choices.

Can I still get financing from a different bank after one rejection?

Often yes. Banks apply their own credit assessment on top of the shared TDSR framework, so a rejection from one lender does not automatically mean every lender will reject you. Common bank specific issues include how a particular lender treats variable income, existing credit facilities, or a marginal TDSR case. A mortgage broker who works across multiple banks can usually tell you quickly whether your file has a realistic path elsewhere or whether the underlying affordability problem is likely to repeat everywhere.

What does the sale and purchase agreement typically say about a financing failure?

Most standard sale and purchase agreements do not build in an automatic financing contingency once the option has been exercised. The completion date is fixed, and a buyer who cannot complete because financing fell through is generally treated the same as any other default, which can put the exercised deposit at risk. Some agreements may include specific clauses relevant to financing, which is exactly why a lawyer needs to review your specific document rather than relying on general assumptions.

Is a rejected loan after exercising the OTP the same as defaulting on a mortgage?

No, these are different problems at different stages. A rejected loan after exercising the OTP happens before completion, when you do not yet own the property, and the risk is losing your deposit and potentially facing further claims under the sale and purchase agreement. Defaulting on a mortgage happens after you already own the property and have an active loan, and the risk is the bank pursuing recovery against the property itself. The immediate steps and the stakes are different, so it is worth being precise about which situation you are actually in.

Can I ask the seller for more time to complete if my loan is delayed?

You can ask, but the seller is under no obligation to agree, and any extension is a negotiated variation to the agreement rather than a right you can claim. Some sellers, particularly if they understand the delay and have no urgent need to complete on the original date, may agree to a short extension, sometimes with a fee attached. Others will hold firmly to the original date. This is a conversation best handled through your lawyer or agent rather than informally, so any agreed extension is properly documented.
