Corner terrace HDB flats: why they command a premium

By Winfred Quek · 7 minute read · Published 13 July 2026

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 13 July 2026

Quick answer: Corner terrace is agent shorthand for a unit sitting at the end of a block or corridor, bordered by open air rather than a neighbour on more than one side. That position often brings extra windows, better cross ventilation, more privacy from fewer shared walls, and sometimes a slightly larger or differently shaped floor plate. The resale premium these units command is generally justified when the unit delivers real light, air and privacy benefits, and less justified when the corner label is doing more marketing work than the actual living difference. This guide explains what to check before paying for it.

Facts verified: 13 July 2026 · Corner unit layout, orientation and premium vary by block and should be assessed individually · Sources attributed below

Ask any agent to point out the best unit on a floor and they will usually walk you to the corner. There is a reason "corner unit" or "corner terrace" shows up so often in resale listings and commands a real price bump. As an investor minded advisor, I want buyers to understand exactly what they are paying for, because the corner premium is sometimes fully earned and sometimes mostly a listing photo.

What agents mean by a corner terrace HDB unit

There is no official HDB flat type called corner terrace. It is a market term for a unit positioned at the end of a block or the end of a corridor, where one or more sides border open air rather than another flat. Because it sits at the edge rather than being sandwiched between two neighbours, a corner unit typically has an external wall where a party wall would otherwise be, and architects tend to use that wall for windows rather than leaving it blank.

The word terrace is added colloquially because these end units, especially in older low rise or point blocks, sometimes have a slightly different floor plate that uses the corner space efficiently, occasionally resulting in a bay window, a wraparound layout, or marginally more floor area than the standard mid corridor unit of the same flat type in the same block.

Why corner units get extra windows and sometimes extra space

The physical logic is simple. A mid corridor flat is flanked by neighbours on both sides, so its only external walls face the front and back of the block. A corner unit has an additional external wall on the side that would otherwise be shared, and that wall usually gets windows, bringing in more natural light and better cross ventilation than a mid corridor unit can achieve.

In some blocks, particularly older estates designed before uniform floor plates became standard, the corner position was also used to fit a slightly larger unit, since the corner of a block often has irregular usable space that a standard mid corridor layout would waste. Not every corner unit is larger, but where it is, that is usually why.

The privacy and noise trade off

Fewer shared walls is the headline privacy benefit of a corner unit. With only one or no neighbour sharing a wall, instead of two, sound transfer from adjoining flats drops meaningfully, which is a genuine quality of life upgrade in dense HDB living. This is the benefit buyers are usually willing to pay the most for, because it is felt every day rather than admired occasionally like a view.

Corner does not always mean quiet. Depending on the block's layout, a corner unit can sit directly beside a lift lobby, a staircase, a rubbish chute point, or face a busier road or carpark. Any of these can offset or even reverse the privacy and noise advantage that "corner unit" implies. The label describes the unit's position on the floor plan, not automatically its actual living experience, and the two need to be checked separately.

Does the resale premium match the actual living difference

This is the honest question every buyer should ask before paying extra for a corner unit, and the answer is genuinely mixed. Where a corner unit delivers real, verifiable benefits, more natural light across more hours of the day, better airflow, fewer shared walls, and a layout free of an awkward lift lobby or chute adjacency, the premium is earned and tends to hold up on resale too, because the next buyer will value the same things.

Where the premium is being charged mostly because the word corner appears in the listing, without the unit actually delivering on light, air or privacy, you are paying for marketing rather than for a living upgrade. This is the same discipline I apply across any resale HDB pricing decision, which I cover more broadly in my HDB resale pricing guide: price the actual attributes of the unit, not the label attached to it.

What to check before paying the corner premium

  1. Visit at different times of day. Light quality, noise levels and airflow all vary by time of day, and a single afternoon viewing will not tell you the full story.
  2. Check what the extra windows actually face. A window facing a busy road, a neighbouring block's blank wall, or a carpark delivers far less benefit than one facing open sky or greenery.
  3. Confirm the unit's exact position relative to lift lobbies, staircases and chutes. A corner unit next to a high traffic building feature can lose most of its privacy advantage.
  4. Compare recent transactions for corner versus mid corridor units in the same or a very similar block. This tells you what the market is actually paying for the position, rather than relying on the agent's asking price alone.

Frequently asked questions

What does corner terrace mean for an HDB flat?

It is agent shorthand, not an official HDB flat type, for a unit sitting at the end of a block or corridor where it borders open air on more than one side rather than being flanked by neighbours on both sides. These units often get extra side windows, sometimes a larger or differently shaped floor plate, and fewer shared internal walls than a typical mid corridor unit.

Why do corner units get more windows or extra space?

Because they sit at the end of a block, corner units often have an external wall on a side that would otherwise be shared with a neighbour, which architects fill with windows rather than a blank party wall. In some blocks the corner unit was also designed with a slightly different floor plate to use the corner space efficiently, resulting in a larger or differently laid out unit than the standard mid corridor flat.

Are corner units noisier or quieter than mid corridor units?

It depends on the block and the corner's orientation. Fewer shared walls generally means less noise transfer from neighbours, which is the main privacy benefit buyers pay for. But a corner unit can also face a busier road, a lift lobby, or a rubbish chute point depending on the block layout, so the noise profile has to be checked unit by unit rather than assumed from the corner label alone.

Is the corner unit premium worth paying?

It depends on how much you value the specific benefits, extra light, ventilation, privacy and sometimes space, relative to how much extra the market is asking for that specific unit. The premium is justified when the unit genuinely delivers those benefits and the asking price is proportionate; it is less justified when a unit is marketed as a corner unit but sits beside a lift lobby, rubbish point or busy road that erodes the actual living advantage.

How do I check if a corner unit's premium is fair?

Compare recent resale transactions for corner units against mid corridor units of the same flat type in the same block or a very similar block nearby, and visit at different times of day to assess light, noise and views for yourself. A fair premium tracks a genuine and verifiable living difference, not just the word corner in a listing.
