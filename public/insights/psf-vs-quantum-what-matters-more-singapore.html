PSF vs quantum: which number should drive your decision

By Winfred Quek · 8 minute read · Published 13 July 2026

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 13 July 2026

Quick answer: PSF, price per square foot, and quantum, the total price, answer different questions and neither is universally the "right" number. PSF is the correct tool when you are comparing similar units of different sizes, within the same development or across comparable projects, because it strips out size and shows you relative value per unit area. Quantum is the correct tool when your decision is bounded by an absolute budget, which is how loan eligibility, downpayment, stamp duty and monthly repayment are actually calculated. Most buyers get into trouble by applying PSF logic to a quantum bound decision, chasing a low PSF unit that is actually too large for their budget, or applying quantum logic to a PSF comparison, assuming a cheaper total price means better value without checking size and quality are held constant.

Almost every buyer I sit down with has already absorbed the habit of quoting PSF, because it is the number that dominates listings, agent chat and property forums. Fewer have stopped to ask what PSF is actually for, and what it quietly hides. This is not a trivial distinction. Reaching for the wrong number at the wrong moment in a decision leads buyers to overpay for the wrong unit, or to walk away from a genuinely good one because the headline number looked worse than it was.

What PSF actually measures

Price per square foot is exactly what it says: the transacted or asking price divided by the unit's floor area in square feet. It is the standard unit of comparison across Singapore property listings and the transaction data published by URA, precisely because it allows you to compare units of different sizes on a like for like basis. A 500 square foot unit and a 1,200 square foot unit cannot be compared meaningfully on total price alone, since one is obviously going to cost more simply by being bigger. PSF strips that size effect out and asks, per unit of space, which is priced richer and which is priced cheaper.

That is a genuinely useful question, and PSF answers it well, but only when the units being compared are otherwise similar. The moment you compare across meaningfully different developments, tenures, ages or locations, PSF stops being a clean apples to apples number and starts blending together several different things at once.

Where PSF is the right tool

PSF earns its keep in a narrow but genuinely important set of comparisons. Within the same development, comparing different stacks, floors and facings on a PSF basis tells you exactly how the market, or the developer, is pricing those differences, and whether a particular unit looks rich or cheap relative to its neighbours. Across a small set of comparable developments in the same micro location, similar tenure and similar age, PSF is a reasonable shorthand for relative value, provided you have actually confirmed the developments are comparable rather than assuming it from proximity alone.

This is also the number that matters most when you are trying to read whether a district or segment is repricing over time, since tracking median PSF trends strips out the noise of individual transactions being for larger or smaller units in any given period. For how to read that kind of transaction data properly, including its real limitations, see my guide to checking property transaction history.

Where quantum takes over

The moment your decision is bounded by an absolute budget, which describes most home purchases, quantum becomes the number that actually governs what you can and cannot do. Your maximum loan quantum under the Total Debt Servicing Ratio, your downpayment in cash and CPF, your Buyer's Stamp Duty and any Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty, and your monthly mortgage repayment are all calculated off the total price, not the per square foot rate. A unit with an attractively low PSF is irrelevant if its total quantum, because it is large, pushes your monthly repayment past what your income can comfortably support.

The trap runs both ways. Buyers chasing a low PSF sometimes end up in a unit that is larger, and therefore more expensive in total, than they actually needed or could comfortably afford, purely because the per square foot number looked like a deal. Equally, buyers anchored on quantum sometimes reject a smaller, higher PSF unit that would have suited them better, simply because the PSF number looks worse on paper, without registering that the total price is actually lower and well within budget. Know which number your decision is actually bounded by before you let either one drive the choice.

A worked framework, not a worked example

Rather than anchoring this to specific figures that would date quickly, here is the framework I actually use with clients. First, establish your quantum ceiling: the maximum total price your financing, downpayment and comfortable monthly repayment can support, worked out properly rather than assumed. That number is fixed and should not move because a listing's PSF looks attractive. Second, within units that clear your quantum ceiling, use PSF to compare relative value between the specific units and developments still on your shortlist. PSF is the tiebreaker within your budget, not the gatekeeper that decides your budget.

What a low PSF can be hiding

A lower PSF than the surrounding comparables is not automatically a bargain, and treating it as one without asking why is the single most common mistake I see in PSF led comparisons. Lower floors, less desirable facings, proximity to a bin centre, main road or transformer, a less efficient layout with more wasted circulation space, and simple distance from the development's own MRT access point or amenities can all suppress PSF for entirely legitimate reasons that have nothing to do with the unit being underpriced. My floor plan efficiency guide covers one of the biggest hidden factors: two units of the identical strata size can deliver very different usable space, which materially affects whether a given PSF is actually cheap.

Before treating a low PSF as a signal to buy, walk through the specific reasons it might be lower, confirm none of them are dealbreakers for your own use case, and only then treat the discount as real value rather than a fair reflection of a genuinely lesser unit.

What a high PSF can be justifying

The same logic runs in reverse. A premium PSF can reflect a genuinely superior unit, a higher floor with an unblocked view, a more efficient layout, direct MRT or amenity access, or simply strong, durable demand for that specific micro location. It can equally reflect a moment of frothy sentiment in a hot launch that cools once the initial excitement passes. The skill is not in memorising which PSF band is "expensive," since that number is meaningless without district and segment context, my CCR RCR OCR framework guide covers how location tier alone explains much of the PSF spread across the island, but in checking whether the specific features commanding the premium are ones that actually matter to you and will hold their value for the next buyer too.

A simple decision sequence

  1. Fix your quantum ceiling first, based on real financing numbers, before you start comparing listings.
  2. Use PSF only to compare within your shortlist, between units that clear your quantum ceiling and are genuinely comparable in tenure, age and location.
  3. Interrogate any outlier PSF, high or low, by naming the specific feature that explains it, rather than assuming it reflects pure value.
  4. Track PSF over time, not price over time, when reading whether a district or project is appreciating, since raw price trends are distorted by the mix of unit sizes transacted in each period.

Frequently asked questions

What is PSF in Singapore property and how is it calculated?

PSF stands for price per square foot, calculated by dividing the transacted or asking price by the unit's floor area in square feet. It is the standard metric used across listings and URA transaction data to compare value on a per unit area basis between units of different sizes.

When should I compare properties by PSF instead of total price?

PSF is most useful comparing genuinely similar units of different sizes, most cleanly within the same development, or across comparable developments in the same micro location and tier. It strips out size so you can see whether one unit is priced richer or cheaper per square foot.

When does total quantum matter more than PSF?

Quantum matters more whenever your decision is bounded by an absolute budget, which describes most home purchases. Loan eligibility, downpayment, stamp duty and monthly repayment are all driven by total price, not the per square foot rate.

Why can a lower PSF still mean a worse deal?

A lower PSF on its own does not explain why it is lower. It could reflect a genuine bargain, or a lower floor, worse facing, less efficient layout, or a less convenient location within the same development. Compare without controlling for these factors and a bargain PSF on a fundamentally worse unit is not actually a bargain.
