Family offices and Singapore property: how the asset actually fits

By Winfred Quek · 10 minute read · Published 9 August 2026

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 9 August 2026

Quick answer: Singapore residential property is a genuinely difficult asset for a family office to hold cleanly. Any company, trust or similar entity buying residential property currently pays Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty at 65 percent, a rate set well above what an individual pays, which is the single fact that dominates the arithmetic for almost every structuring conversation. Commercial and industrial property sit outside that regime and are treated very differently. Foreign ownership of landed residential property is separately restricted under the Residential Property Act, with the Singapore Land Authority as the approving authority. This page describes how the pieces fit together in neutral terms. It is not tax or legal advice, and any actual structuring decision needs a lawyer and a tax adviser working from your family's specific facts.

Family offices in Singapore hold a wide range of assets, listed securities, private equity, cash, and in many cases property. Property is often the asset that generates the most questions, because unlike a share purchase, it interacts with stamp duty, ownership restrictions and financing rules that were built around individual home buyers, not investment entities. This guide sets out how Singapore residential property actually behaves as an asset for an entity to hold, why it is harder than it looks, and how the common holding routes compare. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy, hold or structure anything in particular.

What a single family office actually is, in plain terms

A single family office is a private entity set up to manage the wealth of one family, typically structured as a company that employs or contracts investment and administrative staff to oversee the family's holdings. It is distinct from a multi family office, which serves several unrelated families through a shared platform, and from a private bank, which provides advisory and custody services but does not sit inside the family's own structure.

Singapore has become a common jurisdiction for single family offices because of its regulatory stability, its network of tax treaties, and tax schemes administered by the Monetary Authority of Singapore for qualifying investment income. Those schemes govern how certain investment income is taxed. They are not property schemes, and they do not change the stamp duty a family office pays when it buys Singapore real estate. That distinction, between the income tax treatment of the vehicle and the stamp duty treatment of a specific asset class, is the first thing worth being clear on before any property conversation starts. For scheme conditions and thresholds, MAS and IRAS are the primary sources, not this page.

Why Singapore residential property is a difficult asset for an entity to hold

Singapore's Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty, layered on top of the base Buyer's Stamp Duty, is calibrated by who is buying. A Singapore citizen individual generally pays 0 percent on a first property, rising through 20 percent and 30 percent for subsequent properties. A Permanent Resident individual generally pays 5 percent on a first property, rising to 30 percent and 35 percent. A foreigner buying individually generally pays 60 percent. An entity, a company, a trust, or a similar vehicle, buying residential property currently pays ABSD at 65 percent, regardless of the nationality of the underlying owners or beneficiaries.

That 65 percent rate is the number that dominates the arithmetic for almost any family office considering Singapore residential property. On a meaningfully priced home, it adds a duty bill larger than many families expect, payable upfront at the point of purchase, well before any rental income or later sale can offset it. This is a deliberate policy setting, not an oversight, designed to make anonymous or speculative corporate holding of residential property expensive relative to individual ownership. It is worth being direct about what this means: for most families, buying residential property through an entity is not a way to reduce duty, and structuring around it purely to save on stamp duty is exactly the kind of decision that needs proper legal and tax advice rather than a general framework. My ABSD trust structures guide and the ABSD glossary entry cover the mechanics in more depth, and current rates should always be confirmed directly with IRAS.

Residential versus commercial and industrial: the duty and ownership divide

The 65 percent entity rate applies specifically to residential property. Commercial property, retail and office space, and industrial property sit in a materially different regime. Neither attracts ABSD for any category of buyer, individual or entity, Singapore citizen or foreign. Buyers of commercial and industrial property still pay the base Buyer's Stamp Duty, and foreign ownership restrictions that apply to residential property generally do not apply in the same way to commercial and industrial assets.

This is a large part of why family offices that do want direct Singapore property exposure often look first at commercial or industrial assets rather than residential ones. The duty arithmetic is simply more straightforward, and ownership restrictions are lighter. That said, commercial and industrial property carry their own considerations, usage restrictions, tenancy structures, and a different financing and liquidity profile than residential, and none of it should be read as a suggestion that commercial property is automatically the better choice. It depends entirely on what role the asset is meant to play in the family's wider portfolio, which is a conversation for a property adviser working alongside a tax adviser, not a generic comparison.

Foreign ownership restrictions on landed residential property

Separate from stamp duty, Singapore restricts the ownership of landed residential property, terrace houses, semi detached and detached houses, and bungalows, under the Residential Property Act. A foreign person, and generally an entity that is foreign controlled, needs approval from the Singapore Land Authority before acquiring landed residential property, and approval is not automatic. There are limited categories of landed property, such as within Sentosa Cove, where different rules have historically applied, and these categories and their conditions are set and reviewed by the authorities, not fixed facts that stay the same indefinitely.

Non landed private residential property, condominium and apartment units, is not subject to the same Residential Property Act restriction, though ABSD still applies at the relevant individual or entity rate. This distinction, landed versus non landed, sits alongside the residential versus commercial distinction as a second filter that shapes what a family office or a foreign individual can realistically acquire directly. Because approval processes, designated exceptions and eligibility criteria are set by the Singapore Land Authority and can be updated, anyone considering a landed purchase should confirm the current position with SLA and with legal counsel before proceeding, rather than relying on a general description.

Direct ownership, entity ownership or S REITs: the honest trade offs

There are, broadly, three ways a family can gain exposure to Singapore property: buying directly in an individual's name, buying through a company or trust, or gaining indirect exposure through listed vehicles such as S REITs. Each behaves differently across duty, control, financing, liquidity and succession, and none is universally superior.

Liquidity, concentration and succession considerations

Beyond duty, three practical issues tend to shape whether property fits a family office's portfolio at all. Liquidity is the first: a single Singapore property is a large, indivisible holding that cannot be partially sold to raise cash, and any sale within the Seller's Stamp Duty window, currently four years from purchase with rates stepping down from 16 percent in year one to 4 percent in year four, adds a further layer of cost to an early exit.

Concentration is the second. A single property, especially a landed home or a large residential unit, can represent a disproportionate share of a family's total wealth relative to how diversified a listed portfolio or a basket of REITs would be. That concentration is not automatically wrong, but it is a risk that should be sized and discussed deliberately rather than accumulated by default because a property felt like the obvious thing to buy.

Succession is the third, and often the most consequential over a family's multi generation horizon. A directly held property passes through an individual's estate and is subject to the usual will, intestacy or joint ownership rules. An entity held property can, in principle, allow ownership interests to transfer without a sale of the underlying asset, but this requires careful legal structuring, has its own tax consequences at each transfer, and is not a shortcut around duty. Any family considering property as part of a succession plan should work through the mechanics with a lawyer and a tax adviser well ahead of time, rather than treating the property purchase and the succession plan as separate decisions.

Why property often sits alongside, not inside, the core portfolio

For many Singapore family offices, the practical outcome of this arithmetic is that direct residential property ends up sitting alongside the core investment portfolio, held for the family's own use, for sentimental or lifestyle reasons, or as a small satellite allocation, rather than functioning as a core, actively traded holding inside the entity itself. The 65 percent entity ABSD rate makes frequent entity level trading of residential property expensive in a way that listed securities are not, and the Seller's Stamp Duty window reinforces a longer holding horizon regardless of who owns the asset.

This does not make Singapore property a poor asset. It makes it a different kind of asset, illiquid, concentrated, and duty sensitive in a way that shapes when and how it should sit in a family's broader plan. None of this is a forecast of future prices or returns, and nothing here should be read as a recommendation to buy, hold or avoid any specific property, project or district. Every family's facts, existing structure, citizenship mix, and long term goals are different, which is exactly why this decision belongs with a lawyer and a tax adviser working from those facts, alongside a CEA licensed property adviser for the transaction itself.

Frequently asked questions

What is a single family office in Singapore, in plain terms?

A single family office is a private entity, usually a company, set up to manage the wealth and affairs of one family, as distinct from a multi family office that serves several unrelated families under one roof. It typically employs or contracts investment, tax and administrative staff to oversee the family's investments, which can include listed securities, private equity, cash, and sometimes direct property. The structure and any tax treatment a family office qualifies for are governed by the Monetary Authority of Singapore and IRAS, and the specific conditions should be confirmed with a licensed adviser rather than assumed from general commentary.

Why does Singapore residential property attract a 65 percent ABSD rate for a company or trust?

Singapore applies Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty on top of the base Buyer's Stamp Duty, and any entity, a company, trust or similar vehicle, buying residential property currently pays ABSD at 65 percent regardless of the nationality of its underlying owners or beneficiaries. This entity rate sits well above the rates individuals pay, which range from 0 percent for a Singapore citizen's first property up to 60 percent for a foreigner, and it exists as a policy setting to discourage residential property from being held anonymously or speculatively through corporate structures. The current rate and any remission conditions should always be verified directly with IRAS before a purchase, since duty rates are set by policy and can change.

Can a foreigner or a family office buy landed residential property in Singapore?

Landed residential property, houses, terraces, bungalows and similar, is restricted under the Residential Property Act, and a foreign person or a foreign controlled entity generally needs approval from the Singapore Land Authority before acquiring it, with limited exceptions such as designated developments like Sentosa Cove. Non landed private property, condominiums and apartments, does not carry the same restriction, though ABSD still applies. Anyone considering a landed purchase as a foreign national or through an entity should confirm current eligibility and the approval process directly with the Singapore Land Authority.

Should a family office hold Singapore property personally or through an entity?

There is no universally correct answer, and this is precisely the kind of decision that should not be made from a general article. Personal ownership is usually simpler and avoids the 65 percent entity ABSD rate, but ties the asset to an individual's personal balance sheet and estate. Entity ownership can support governance and succession planning goals but carries the higher duty rate and added compliance cost. The right structure depends on the family's full financial and legal picture, and should be decided together with a lawyer and a tax adviser, not on duty rate alone.
