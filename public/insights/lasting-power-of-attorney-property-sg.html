Lasting Power of Attorney and Property in Singapore: What to Set Up Before You Can't

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · PropNex Realty

Quick answer: An LPA allows your chosen donee to manage your property and financial affairs if you lose mental capacity. Without an LPA, your family cannot sell or refinance your property on your behalf — they must apply to court (months of delay, $3,000–$8,000 in costs). LPA Form 1 covers property/financial matters. Registration at OPG costs $75 (waived for Merdeka/Pioneer generation).

What Is an LPA?

A Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA) is a legal document under the Mental Capacity Act that lets you appoint a trusted person (the "donee") to make decisions and take actions on your behalf if you lose mental capacity. It must be set up while you still have mental capacity — once lost, it is too late.

The LPA covers two domains (you choose which to include):

  • Property and financial affairs: sell property, manage bank accounts, collect rent, pay bills, invest
  • Personal welfare: medical decisions, living arrangements, care choices

Most people set up LPAs covering both. For property purposes, the property and financial affairs section is essential.

What the LPA Donee Can Do with Your Property

  • Sell or transfer your property on your behalf
  • Refinance your mortgage
  • Collect and manage rental income from your investment property
  • Pay property tax, maintenance fees, and other property-related expenses
  • Grant tenancies and renew leases
  • Apply to HDB for flat-related transactions on your behalf

HDB explicitly recognises and accepts LPAs for processing property transactions when the owner lacks mental capacity.

Requirements

  • Donor (you): Must be 21+, must have mental capacity at time of signing
  • Donee (your appointee): Must be 21+, not an undischarged bankrupt, not a paid professional unless Form 2
  • Certificate issuer: Accredited GP, specialist doctor, or lawyer must certify donor has capacity and understands the LPA
  • Registration: Must be registered with OPG (Office of the Public Guardian) to take effect

Critical distinction: An LPA operates during your lifetime — it covers the period when you are alive but mentally incapacitated. For death, you need a separate Will (and CPF nomination). An LPA does not replace a Will. You need both.

How to Set One Up

  1. Decide on your donee(s) and the powers you wish to grant
  2. Visit a GP or lawyer to complete the certificate issuer section (certifying your mental capacity)
  3. Complete LPA Form 1 (available at opg.mlaw.gov.sg)
  4. Submit for OPG registration ($75, or waived for qualifying Pioneer/Merdeka Generation)
  5. Registration typically processed within 3 weeks

What Most People Get Wrong About LPAs

  • Waiting until it's too late. An LPA must be set up while you have mental capacity. A dementia diagnosis or stroke can make you ineligible to execute an LPA overnight. The time to act is now, not after a medical event.
  • Thinking a Will covers this. A Will takes effect only on death. An LPA operates during your lifetime when you are alive but incapacitated. You need both documents — they cover different situations.
  • Not appointing a substitute donee. If your primary donee predeceases you or is unable to act, an LPA without a substitute donee becomes ineffective. Always name at least one substitute donee, especially for property matters.
  • Granting unlimited powers without safeguards. LPA Form 1 grants broad general powers. Consider whether you want to restrict the donee from gifting your property or making certain investments. A Form 2 LPA lets you customise restrictions, albeit at higher cost.
  • Forgetting to tell your donee where the LPA document is. An LPA that cannot be located is useless in an emergency. Lodge a certified copy with your lawyer and inform your donee of its location.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my donee sell my property to themselves under an LPA?

No. An LPA donee has a fiduciary duty to act in your best interests and cannot enter into transactions that benefit themselves at your expense. Selling your property to the donee personally would be a conflict of interest and is legally challengeable. The Office of the Public Guardian can investigate and revoke the LPA if the donee acts improperly.

Does an LPA expire?

An LPA does not have an expiry date once registered with OPG. It remains in effect until you revoke it (while you still have capacity), the donee renounces the appointment, or the donee is unable to act (e.g. dies or becomes bankrupt). You can revoke your LPA at any time while you retain mental capacity.

Can I have more than one donee under one LPA?

Yes. You can appoint multiple donees and specify whether they must act jointly (both must agree on every decision) or jointly and severally (each can act independently). For property transactions, jointly and severally is more practical — it allows either donee to sign documents without requiring both to be physically present.

Is a Power of Attorney the same as an LPA?

No. A regular Power of Attorney (POA) is used for specific, time-limited transactions — for example, authorising an agent to complete a property purchase while you are overseas. A POA automatically becomes invalid if you lose mental capacity. An LPA is specifically designed to survive loss of mental capacity — that is its entire purpose and what distinguishes it from a regular POA.
