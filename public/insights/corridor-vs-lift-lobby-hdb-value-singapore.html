Does unit position within a block affect an HDB flat's value?

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 13 July 2026

Quick answer: Yes, though the effect is smaller and more design dependent than floor level or facing direction. A corridor facing unit generally loses a little privacy and picks up more foot noise, a unit right beside the lift lobby trades convenience for motor and traffic noise, and an end unit often gains an extra exposed wall for light and ventilation, if that wall does not simply face a bin centre or blank wall. None of these factors move value on their own the way a high floor or a good direction does. They matter most when you are choosing between several units in the same stack and floor band, where they can be the deciding factor between two flats that look identical on paper.

Most conversations about HDB resale value stop at two questions: what floor, and which way does it face. Both matter enormously, and rightly get the attention. But anyone who has sat through enough viewings knows there is a second layer of decisions happening inside a single stack, on a single floor, that a floor and direction filter cannot capture. Two four room units on the same floor, same stack, same block, can feel completely different to live in because of where they sit relative to the corridor, the lift lobby, and the end of the block. This guide is about that second layer, the part buyers often notice only after they have already shortlisted a flat.

Why block position gets overlooked

Portal listings and resale price analyses are built around floor level and orientation because those are the two variables that are consistent across every unit in a stack and easy to compare across an entire estate. Corridor exposure, lift lobby distance and end unit status are block specific and sometimes even floor specific within the same block, so they resist the kind of clean averaging that produces a district level price trend. That does not mean they are unimportant, only that they do not show up cleanly in the aggregate numbers the way floor and direction do. They live in the walkthrough, not the spreadsheet.

The practical consequence is that these factors get priced by individual buyers reacting to what they see and hear on viewing day, rather than by a market wide convention. That makes them a source of genuine negotiating room, in both directions, for a buyer who pays attention and a seller who does not realise what they are giving up or overselling.

Corridor facing units: what actually changes

In many HDB block designs, particularly older slab blocks, the common corridor runs along one face of the building and units open directly onto it. If your living room or kitchen windows face that corridor, you lose a measure of privacy, since passersby can glance in, and you pick up ambient noise from foot traffic, neighbours' conversations and the occasional trolley being wheeled past. On a low or mid floor with a busy corridor, this is the kind of thing a buyer notices within the first minute of a viewing.

The severity varies a great deal by block typology. Newer point blocks and many Build To Order designs use wing corridors, sky terraces, or layouts where the corridor only runs past bedrooms or bathrooms rather than the living and dining area, which sharply reduces the practical impact. Some designs even set the corridor back from the unit facade with a buffer, or use single loaded corridors that only serve two or three units per landing. Before assuming a corridor facing unit carries a meaningful discount, look at exactly which rooms face the corridor and how many households share that stretch of walkway.

Lift lobby proximity: convenience versus intrusion

Being close to the lift is one of those preferences that looks purely positive until you actually live it. Yes, you cut your walk down to seconds, which matters more than people expect once you are carrying groceries, moving furniture, or helping an elderly parent or a child in a pram. But the unit closest to the lift also absorbs the mechanical hum of the lift motor, the arrival chime, and doubles as an informal waiting and gathering spot for every other household on the floor heading in or out.

At the other extreme, a unit at the far end of a long corridor gets genuine quiet and reduced traffic, at the cost of a longer daily walk that becomes a real friction point for households with mobility needs. In practice, most buyers I speak with settle on a preference for the middle ground, a few doors from the lift lobby rather than immediately adjacent to it or at the very end of the block. That middle position tends to be the least contested and the easiest to sell on again later, precisely because it avoids both extremes.

End units: the quiet premium

End units sit at the tip of a block or wing and typically have one fewer party wall than a middle unit in the same stack, which can translate into an additional window or exposed face. That extra opening often means better cross ventilation and more natural light reaching rooms that would otherwise be enclosed on three sides. Agents frequently flag end units as a selling point for exactly this reason, and buyers who value natural light and airflow, which in Singapore's climate is not a small thing, will often pay a touch more for it.

Check what the exposed wall actually faces. The end unit advantage depends entirely on what sits beyond that extra wall. An end unit that opens onto a quiet side garden or unobstructed view is genuinely different from one that faces a bin centre, a substation, a blank neighbouring block wall, or a busy access road. Walk the exterior of the block, not just the interior of the unit, before deciding an end unit is worth a premium.

How agents and valuers actually weigh position

Bank valuations for HDB flats are built primarily from comparable transactions filtered by block, floor level range, and floor area, with adjustments for renovation condition and, where relevant, remaining lease. Corridor exposure, lift proximity and end unit status are not standard line items in that process the way floor level bands are; they surface indirectly, through which specific comparable transactions a valuer selects and how buyers in the open market have already priced similar units. This is one reason a bank valuation can occasionally come in below or above what feels intuitively right for a specific unit's position within its block, a mismatch I cover in more depth in my bank valuation lower than purchase price guide.

In my own experience walking clients through resale flats, position within the block tends to function as a tiebreaker rather than a headline driver. When two units are otherwise similar in floor, direction and condition, the one with better corridor privacy, sensible lift distance and a genuinely favourable end unit exposure will usually attract a slightly faster sale and a marginally firmer price, even if that premium never gets isolated as its own line item in any official statistic.

Putting it together: a buyer's checklist

  1. Walk the corridor before you walk the unit. Note which rooms face it, how wide it is, and how much foot traffic passes at different times of day if you can view more than once.
  2. Time your lift lobby visit. A quiet lift lobby at 11am on a weekday can be a very different place at 7pm when everyone is coming home from work and school.
  3. Confirm what an end unit's extra wall faces. A side view of greenery is not the same as a side view of a service yard, even though both technically qualify as an end unit.
  4. Weigh position against your own daily pattern. A family with young children or elderly parents will value lift proximity differently from a working couple who is rarely home during the day.
  5. Use position as a negotiating lever, not a dealbreaker. A less than ideal corridor exposure on an otherwise excellent unit is often a fair basis to ask for a modest price adjustment rather than a reason to walk away entirely.

None of this replaces the fundamentals. Floor level, direction, remaining lease and overall condition still do the heavy lifting in determining what an HDB flat is worth, a framework I go through in detail in my HDB resale pricing guide. Block position is the layer that decides which specific unit, among several that look equally good on paper, ends up being the right one for how you actually live.

Frequently asked questions

Does a corridor facing HDB unit sell for less?

Often yes, though the discount is modest and depends heavily on how the corridor is designed. A unit whose main windows and kitchen face a common corridor loses some privacy and can pick up foot traffic noise, and buyers who notice this during a viewing will factor it into their offer. Newer point block and Build To Order designs with wing corridors, sky terraces or corridors that only face bedrooms rather than living areas narrow this gap considerably, so the effect is design specific rather than a fixed percentage.

Is it better to be near or far from the lift lobby?

Both extremes have a cost. Units immediately next to the lift lobby get convenience but absorb lift motor noise, the ding of arrival, and passing traffic from every household on the floor. Units at the far end of a long corridor get quiet and privacy but a longer walk with groceries, prams or an elderly parent using a walking frame. Most buyers land on a preference for being a few doors from the lift, close enough for convenience, far enough to lose the noise and traffic.

Do end units really command a premium?

End units are frequently sought after because they typically have one fewer shared wall, which can mean better natural light, cross ventilation and quieter living. Whether that translates into a measurable premium depends on the specific block layout; some end units back onto a bin centre, substation or blank wall that erases the advantage. The premium is real often enough that agents flag end units as a selling point, but a buyer should verify what the unit's exposed side actually faces before assuming it commands extra value.

Should I pay more attention to floor level or block position?

Floor level and direction remain the larger, more consistently priced factors in HDB resale, because they affect nearly every unit in a stack the same way. Block position is a second order factor that adjusts value within a given floor and stack rather than driving it. Use floor and direction to shortlist a stack, then use corridor exposure, lift proximity and end unit status to choose between the specific units available in that stack.
