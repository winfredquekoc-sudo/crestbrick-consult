Running for or voting in your MCST council

By Winfred Quek · 8 minute read · Published 13 July 2026

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 13 July 2026

Quick answer: Any registered owner, or an authorised representative of a corporate owner, is generally eligible to stand for their condo's management council, which is elected at the AGM and holds real influence over maintenance fees, sinking fund adequacy, and how the managing agent is directed. The time commitment ranges from an occasional meeting in a quiet year to a much heavier load during a major project or dispute. You do not need to stand yourself to have influence, attending the AGM, submitting a proxy, and voting thoughtfully on who does stand is often enough. This guide covers what the role actually involves and how to decide whether it is worth your time.

Every condo has a small group of owners who quietly decide most of what happens to the building for the year ahead: what the maintenance fee will be, whether the sinking fund gets topped up properly, which contractor gets the facade repainting job, and how disputes get handled. That group is the management council, elected at the AGM, usually by a fraction of the owners who bothered to show up. If you have ever wondered whether standing for that council, or simply voting more deliberately in the election, is worth the effort, this is the honest version of what the role involves.

What the council is actually for

The management council is the body elected by owners to act on behalf of the MCST between general meetings. It sets direction on budgets, approves expenditure within its authorised limits, oversees the managing agent's performance, and makes the judgment calls that inevitably arise between one AGM and the next, whether that is a maintenance emergency, a contractor dispute, or a resident complaint that needs a decision. It does not do the hands on work itself; that is the managing agent's job. The council's role is closer to a board of directors than to an operations team, setting priorities and holding the professionals accountable rather than fixing the lift personally.

Who can stand, and the basic process

Eligibility to stand for council election generally extends to any subsidiary proprietor, meaning a registered unit owner, or an authorised representative where the unit is owned by a company, subject to conflict of interest and other provisions in the Building Maintenance and Strata Management Act and the development's own bylaws. Tenants, not being owners, are not eligible to stand, since council membership is tied to ownership rather than residency. Nominations are typically called for ahead of or at the AGM itself, and election is by vote of the owners present or represented by proxy at that meeting.

What the time commitment actually looks like

This is the question I get asked most often by clients weighing whether to put their name forward, and the honest answer is that it varies enormously. In a quiet year, with no major capital works underway and a competent managing agent handling routine matters smoothly, council involvement might mean a meeting every month or two, lasting an hour or two, plus the occasional email thread to approve a decision between meetings. That is a manageable commitment for most working owners.

The commitment spikes during major projects and disputes. A development mid way through a lift replacement, facade repainting, or a managing agent transition, or dealing with a contentious dispute among owners, will demand considerably more of its council members during that period, sometimes including site visits, additional meetings, and fielding resident concerns directly. Anyone considering standing should ask the outgoing council candidly what the current workload actually looks like, rather than assuming it will be light.

Why the council's decisions matter more than owners assume

The people elected to a council shape outcomes that touch every owner's wallet and quality of life, whether or not those owners were paying attention at the AGM. A council inclined to defer maintenance to keep fees visibly low in the short term produces a very different building, five and ten years out, than one that prioritises steady, adequately funded upkeep. I cover the financial mechanics of exactly this trade off, and how to spot it as a buyer, in my sinking fund versus management fund guide. The council is also the body most directly responsible for how consistently bylaws get enforced, a topic covered in my condo bylaws guide, since inconsistent enforcement is often a council capacity issue as much as a rules issue.

Deciding whether to stand

  1. Be honest about your available time. A council seat taken on and then neglected is arguably worse for the building than leaving the seat to someone with more bandwidth.
  2. Understand what is actually delegated to the managing agent. If your development has a strong, well run managing agent, the council's job leans more toward oversight and judgment calls than hands on operational involvement.
  3. Talk to the outgoing council before committing. Ask what the last year actually involved, including any disputes, major projects, or managing agent issues, so you are deciding with real information rather than assumptions.
  4. Consider what you would actually change. If you have a specific concern, fee levels, sinking fund adequacy, enforcement consistency, standing for council is one of the more direct ways to influence it, more so than raising it as a comment from the floor each year.

If you would rather just vote well

Not everyone needs to stand to have a meaningful voice. Attending the AGM, or submitting a proxy if you genuinely cannot make it, and actually reading the notice of meeting before you vote, rather than rubber stamping whatever is proposed, is a low effort way to influence outcomes without taking on the ongoing time commitment of a council seat. Paying attention to who is standing, and asking a candidate directly what their priorities are before you vote for them, matters just as much as the vote itself. A council is only as good as the owners who bothered to choose it carefully.

Frequently asked questions

Who can stand for a condo's MCST council?

Generally, any subsidiary proprietor, meaning a registered unit owner, or an authorised representative of a corporate owner, is eligible to stand for election to the management council at the AGM, subject to specific eligibility and conflict of interest provisions set out in the Building Maintenance and Strata Management Act and the development's bylaws. Tenants are not eligible to stand, since council membership is tied to ownership, not residency.

How much time does being on an MCST council actually take?

It varies considerably by development size, the number of ongoing projects, and how much is delegated to the managing agent. A quiet, well run development might involve a monthly or bimonthly council meeting of an hour or two, plus occasional email decisions between meetings. A development mid way through a major upgrading project, a dispute, or a managing agent transition can demand significantly more time from council members during that period.

Does being on the council mean I do the maintenance work myself?

No. The managing agent, a professional property management firm appointed by the MCST, handles the day to day operational and administrative work, including coordinating contractors, collecting fees, and managing correspondence. The council's role is oversight and decision making, setting direction, approving budgets and major expenditure, and holding the managing agent accountable, not performing hands on maintenance tasks.

If I do not want to stand, how do I still influence council decisions?

Attending the AGM, or submitting a proxy if you cannot attend, and actually reading the notice of meeting and resolutions before voting is the most direct way to influence outcomes without standing yourself. You can also raise concerns directly with existing council members or the managing agent between meetings, and vote thoughtfully on who does stand, since the people elected will make most decisions on your behalf for the following term.
