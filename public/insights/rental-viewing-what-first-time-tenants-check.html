What first time tenants should check at a rental viewing

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · Published 29 August 2026

Quick answer: At a viewing, work room by room rather than relying on a general impression. Check water pressure and the heater in the bathroom, the aircon's age and last servicing, any sign of mould in poorly ventilated corners, mobile signal, noise levels, and which way the unit faces for afternoon sun. Cross check the furniture against the listed inventory, note existing damage on the spot, and ask directly about servicing responsibilities and rental approval before you leave. Bring a written checklist, memory is not reliable across several viewings in one day.

Facts verified: August 2026 · Sources linked below

Key Takeaways

  • • Work through the unit room by room against a written checklist, not from memory after several viewings.
  • • Aircon age, last servicing date, and any mould in unventilated corners are the two most commonly missed checks.
  • • Check mobile signal and stand still to listen for noise, both are easy to miss in a rushed walkthrough.
  • • Photograph existing damage and cross check furniture against the listed inventory before you move in.
  • • If you are renting a single room, ask about access rules, shared space hours, and the guest policy.

Your shortlist from Part 3 gets you to the door. What happens once you are inside decides whether the unit is actually livable, and whether you walk into a dispute six months from now over something you could have caught today. This part sets out a practical, room by room checklist for a first time tenant, along with what to ask the landlord or agent on the spot.

Bring a written checklist

After two or three viewings in a single afternoon, units blur together. A short written checklist, on your phone or on paper, keeps every unit compared on the same basis and stops a good first impression from covering up a real problem you noticed but forgot by the next viewing.

Room by room, what to actually check

Bathroom

Turn on the tap and the shower to feel the actual water pressure, not just whether water comes out. Ask to test the water heater if it is safe to do so. Look at grout lines and ceiling corners for early mould, a sign of poor ventilation, and check that the exhaust fan actually runs and extracts air.

Aircon

Ask how old each unit is and when it was last serviced. Turn each one on if possible and listen for unusual noise or a weak airflow. This detail matters beyond comfort, because many tenancy agreements make the tenant responsible for servicing during the lease, a cost covered in Part 2 of this series.

Bedrooms and living areas

Check natural light and which way the unit faces. A unit facing west catches the harshest afternoon sun, which raises aircon load and fades furniture and flooring faster over the lease. Look again at ceiling and wall corners, especially in rooms with no window, for the same mould and ventilation signs as the bathroom.

Mobile signal and noise

Check your own phone's signal strength in every room, particularly if you plan to work from home, some older or high rise concrete buildings have real dead spots. Stand still for a minute in the living area and bedrooms and actually listen: road noise, void deck activity, and neighbouring units all carry differently depending on the time of day, so factor in when you visited.

Appliances and furniture

Ask the age and condition of the fridge, washing machine, and water heater, and who is responsible for repairs if one fails through normal use, this usually sits with the landlord unless the tenant caused the damage. Cross check the furniture actually in the unit against what the listing or inventory claims, and note any existing damage now. This record is exactly the kind of documentation that protects your security deposit later.

Common areas, and rooms vs whole units

Check the condition of the shared corridor and the letterbox, and confirm it has a working lock and key. If you are renting a room in a shared flat, ask about access rules for the kitchen and bathroom, realistic usable hours, who the other housemates are, and the guest policy. If you are renting a whole unit, ask how the outgoing tenant's utilities account will be closed and yours opened, so you are not stuck with someone else's final bill.

A practical viewing checklist. Adjust for your own priorities and the unit type.

Ask before you leave, not after you sign. Confirm the unit is properly approved for rental, ask what the minor repair cost cap will be, and ask directly who handles aircon servicing going forward. Verbal answers at a viewing are a starting point, get anything that matters written into the tenancy agreement, covered in Part 5 of this series.

Winfred's Take

A viewing is the one chance you get to inspect a unit before you are financially committed to it, and most first time tenants spend that time looking at the sofa instead of the ceiling corners. Take photos of everything, including things that look fine, because fine today is your evidence if it stops being fine in six months. The five extra minutes this takes at a viewing is the cheapest insurance you will buy in the entire process.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most commonly missed check at a rental viewing?

The aircon's last servicing date and any sign of mould in poorly ventilated corners. Both are easy to miss on a quick walkthrough and expensive or unhealthy to discover after you have already moved in.

Should I view a unit more than once before deciding?

Where possible, yes, since a second viewing at a different time of day can reveal noise or sun exposure that a single visit will not show you. It is not always practical, especially on a fast moving unit, but for a lease you plan to sign for a year or more it is worth asking for.

What should I ask about the aircon at a viewing?

Ask how old the units are, when they were last serviced, and who will be responsible for servicing once you move in. This detail matters because many tenancy agreements make the tenant responsible for regular servicing during the lease.

Is mould in a rental unit something to raise immediately?

Yes. Ventilation related mould is far easier to resolve as a condition attached to the tenancy before you sign than to argue about once you are already living there. Point it out at the viewing and ask how the landlord intends to address it.

What should I check if I am renting a single room rather than a whole unit?

Ask about access rules for shared spaces, the hours the kitchen and bathroom are realistically usable, the number and profile of existing housemates, and the guest policy. These day to day details matter as much as the room itself when you are sharing a home.
