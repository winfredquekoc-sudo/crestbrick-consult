Why Singapore tenants find the right rental unit faster with an agent

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · Published 4 September 2026

Quick answer: A tenant searching alone in Singapore typically relies on the two major public portals, PropertyGuru and 99.co, and contacts each listing one by one. An agent works alongside those same portals but also draws on a network of landlords and other agents, some of whom share a new listing with their contacts before it is posted publicly. Just as importantly, an agent checks a unit against the rules that actually govern who can rent it, the minimum lease length, HDB approval, and quota limits, before you spend an evening viewing something you were never eligible to take.

Facts verified: 4 September 2026 · Sources linked below

Key Takeaways

  • • PropertyGuru and 99.co are the two major public rental portals in Singapore, and every tenant, solo or agent assisted, ends up using them.
  • • An agent's network of landlords and other agents is an additional channel that runs alongside the public portals, not a replacement for them.
  • • Private residential leases have a legal minimum term of three consecutive months; anything shorter is short term accommodation and is illegal under URA rules.
  • • HDB flats carry their own rules a tenant should check before committing: HDB's approval requirement, the minimum rental period, and a quota on non Malaysian non citizen tenants.
  • • An agent representing the tenant typically verifies these rules and the landlord's authority to rent before arranging a viewing, not after.

Almost every rental search in Singapore starts the same way: open PropertyGuru or 99.co, filter by district and budget, and start messaging listing agents. That is not a bad way to start. It is simply an incomplete one. What a solo tenant search usually misses is not a better filter, it is a second channel of supply and a layer of checking that happens before, not after, a viewing is booked.

How a solo tenant search actually works

A tenant searching without representation is, in effect, running their own one person agency. Every listing has to be found, read, and followed up individually, usually with a different agent representing each landlord. There is no single point of contact coordinating viewings, no one flagging that a unit's asking rent is out of line with what is actually transacting nearby, and no one checking, before you fall in love with a flat, whether you are even allowed to rent it under that particular arrangement.

This is workable. Plenty of tenants do it. But it means the tenant is carrying the full administrative load of the search: chasing replies, comparing units on memory rather than a shared shortlist, and negotiating each viewing time separately with agents who are, quite reasonably, representing the landlord's interest rather than the tenant's.

What an agent adds to the search

An agent representing a tenant still uses PropertyGuru and 99.co. Those portals remain the widest public inventory of what is available, and no serious search skips them. What an agent adds is a second source: a network of landlords, property managers, and other agents built up over years of transactions. It is common industry practice for a landlord to tell their own agent about a new listing before it goes out to the public portals, sometimes by a day or two, precisely so the agent's own client list gets first look. That is not a guaranteed head start on every unit, and it is not something a tenant can rely on for every search. It is simply an extra channel that a represented tenant has access to and a solo tenant does not. An agent also has a working read on the current state of Singapore's rental market, which is what tells you whether a specific asking rent is realistic before you commit time to viewing it.

The second thing an agent adds is screening. Before a unit reaches your viewing list, an agent checks whether it is one you can actually take: is this an HDB flat, and if so has the flat owner already secured HDB's approval to sublet, does the tenant profile fit within the quota on tenants who are neither Singapore citizens nor Malaysian citizens, and does the listed lease length actually meet the legal minimum. A solo tenant usually only discovers a problem like this after viewing the unit, sometimes after paying an option fee.

Why eligibility checking matters before you view a unit

Singapore's rental rules are not the same for every property type, and getting them wrong costs a tenant time, and sometimes a deposit. According to HDB, an HDB flat can only be sublet with HDB's prior approval, the flat owner must meet the minimum occupation requirements before subletting, and a quota limits how many tenants in a given block or neighbourhood can be citizens of a country other than Singapore or Malaysia. According to URA, a private residential property cannot legally be rented for less than three consecutive months; a shorter stay is short term accommodation, which is illegal, and URA states that an individual found doing this faces a fine starting at $5,000, with heavier penalties in court for repeated or larger scale cases.

None of this is complicated once you know it. The problem is that a solo tenant usually does not find out until they are already interested in a specific unit, sometimes after money has changed hands. An agent's job, before you ever step inside a flat, is to have already ruled out the units that would put you in that position.

Common mistakes tenants make searching alone

  • Assuming a short lease is automatically legal. A landlord offering a two month private rental, or a sublet with no HDB approval, is offering an arrangement that puts the tenant at risk if it is ever discovered, regardless of how reasonable the price looks.
  • Not verifying who they are actually renting from. A solo tenant rarely checks that the person listing a unit has the authority to rent it out, whether that is ownership, HDB approval, or the consent of a co owner.
  • Treating the first available unit as the only option. Without a coordinated shortlist, tenants under time pressure often commit to the first workable unit rather than comparing it against others found the same week.
  • Finding the unit, then discovering the agreement is thin. A great unit with a vague or missing tenancy agreement clause on the deposit or repairs is not actually a good outcome. The search and the agreement matter equally.
  • Missing units shared only within an agent's own network. Not every unit reaches the public portals on day one, and a solo search only sees what has already been posted.
A red flag worth knowing: if a deal looks unusually cheap for a private property because the landlord offers a lease under three months, or an HDB unit because subletting approval has not gone through HDB yet, the saving is not real. The arrangement itself is not legal, and the tenant, not just the landlord, is exposed if it comes to light.

Winfred's Take

I do not tell tenants to stop using PropertyGuru or 99.co. They should. What I add on top is the part that does not show up on a listing page: whether the unit is actually legal to rent the way it is being offered, whether the person listing it has the standing to do so, and whether it is worth your evening before you book a viewing. A search that only looks at price and photos is missing exactly the two things that cause problems later, eligibility and authority to rent. Check those first, then let price and location decide between the units that actually clear the bar.

Frequently asked questions

Do agents really see rental listings before they go public?

Sometimes. It is common industry practice for a landlord to tell their own agent about a new listing before it is posted to PropertyGuru or 99.co, so the agent's client list sees it first. This is not guaranteed on every unit and depends on the individual landlord and agent, but it is a genuine extra channel a represented tenant has access to.

Can I search for a rental unit on my own without an agent?

Yes. Nothing stops a tenant from searching PropertyGuru and 99.co directly and contacting listing agents themselves. The trade off is that the tenant carries the full workload of the search and the eligibility checking alone, rather than having someone screen units before a viewing is arranged.

Does using an agent cost the tenant anything?

Fee arrangements vary by transaction and should always be agreed and put in writing before you commit to working with an agent. This is a question worth asking upfront rather than assuming either way.

What is the difference between PropertyGuru or 99.co and an agent's network?

PropertyGuru and 99.co are public portals open to anyone searching. An agent's network is a set of relationships with landlords and other agents built over time, which can surface a unit before it is posted publicly, and it comes with someone checking the unit's eligibility on your behalf.

How do I know if an HDB unit is actually eligible for me to rent?

According to HDB, the flat owner needs prior approval to sublet, the flat must meet minimum occupation requirements, and a quota applies to tenants who are neither Singapore citizens nor Malaysian citizens. An agent checks these points before shortlisting a unit; a solo tenant should ask the landlord directly and confirm before committing.
