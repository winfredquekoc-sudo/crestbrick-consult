What happens to the mortgage when one co-borrower wants out

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 13 July 2026

Quick answer: Getting one co-borrower off a joint home loan is not a form you fill in, it is a refinancing exercise that requires the bank's fresh approval, all parties' consent, and a separate conveyancing transfer of the title itself. The remaining borrower must pass a standalone Total Debt Servicing Ratio test on the full outstanding loan using only their own income, without counting the exiting party's earnings. If they cannot clear that test alone, the realistic paths are bringing in a replacement co-borrower, paying the loan down to a size that clears TDSR solo, or selling the property and splitting proceeds. This applies to any joint purchase, unmarried couples, friends, or siblings who bought together, not just the spousal decoupling scenario, which carries its own separate stamp duty planning rules.

A good number of the co-ownership questions I field never make it into headlines because they involve no marriage, no divorce lawyer, and no dramatic backstory. Two friends bought a resale flat together in their late twenties because it made financial sense at the time. A couple bought a condo before getting married and later separated. Siblings pooled savings for an investment unit and one of them now wants to cash out. In every case the question is the same: how do we actually get one person off this loan, and what does it cost to do it cleanly?

Two separate things: the loan and the title

The first thing to understand is that your mortgage and your property title are two different legal relationships, and unwinding one does not automatically unwind the other. The loan is a contract between the borrowers and the bank. Every co-borrower on a joint loan typically carries joint and several liability, meaning the bank can pursue any one of you, or all of you, for the full outstanding balance regardless of how you privately agreed to split repayments. The title, separately, reflects who legally owns the property, whether as joint tenants or tenants in common, and in what shares.

Exiting the loan requires the bank's consent through a refinancing exercise. Exiting the title requires a conveyancing transfer, with its own legal process and its own stamp duty. Most co-borrower exits need to execute both, in a carefully sequenced order, usually with the title transfer and the loan refinance completing around the same time so the remaining owner ends up holding both full ownership and full liability for the debt cleanly.

The standalone TDSR test the remaining borrower must clear

This is where most co-borrower exits actually succeed or fail. When the remaining party wants to take over the full loan solo, the bank does not simply relabel the existing loan. It runs a fresh assessment, checking whether that one person's income alone clears the Total Debt Servicing Ratio, currently capped at 55 percent of gross monthly income across all debt obligations, with a stress test applied at a 4 percent interest rate floor regardless of the actual contracted rate on the loan.

The exiting co-borrower's income, which may have made the original joint application comfortable, disappears from this calculation entirely. A loan that was easily affordable for two incomes can fail decisively on one. This is the single most common reason a co-borrower exit does not proceed as smoothly as the parties hoped, and it is worth stress testing before either party makes any promises to the other.

The stamp duty on transferring the exiting party's share

Because this is not a spousal transaction, none of the exemptions or remissions available to married couples decoupling for stamp duty planning purposes apply here. Buyer's Stamp Duty is payable by the remaining owner on the value of the share being acquired, calculated under the standard progressive tiers. If the remaining owner already holds another property, Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty may also apply to the acquired share depending on their profile and existing property count under the current ABSD schedule. This is a real cost that needs to be priced into the negotiation between the parties, not treated as a formality.

CPF refunds complicate the arithmetic. If either party used CPF Ordinary Account savings toward the original purchase or the mortgage instalments, CPF requires a refund of the amount used plus accrued interest back into the exiting owner's CPF account before that share can be legally transferred. This refund reduces the cash the exiting party actually walks away with, and it needs to be modelled before either side agrees on a buyout price, not discovered afterward.

Sequencing the exit correctly

  1. Agree on a current valuation. Commission an independent valuation, or use bank valuation as a reference point, so the buyout price for the exiting share is grounded in fact rather than negotiation pressure.
  2. Confirm the remaining borrower's standalone eligibility first. There is no point agreeing terms before checking whether the bank will actually approve a solo refinance at the required quantum.
  3. Engage a conveyancing lawyer for the title transfer. This runs in parallel with the loan refinance and needs to complete in the correct legal sequence so ownership and liability change hands together.
  4. Settle CPF refunds before completion. Both parties should know their net cash position, after CPF refund and stamp duty, before signing anything binding.
  5. Discharge and re lodge the mortgage. The bank will discharge the original joint loan and lodge a new one in the remaining borrower's sole name, or in the name of the remaining and new co-borrower if one is brought in.

When the remaining borrower cannot qualify alone

This is the scenario nobody plans for at purchase but that comes up often enough to plan for anyway. If the standalone TDSR test fails, the remaining borrower cannot simply wish their way past it, income is income and the stress test floor does not bend. The realistic options are a replacement co-borrower who is willing to take on joint liability, typically a parent or sibling under a joint borrower structure, a partial prepayment that shrinks the outstanding loan enough to clear TDSR on one income, or accepting that the property needs to be sold, with both parties splitting the net proceeds after the loan is discharged and any Seller's Stamp Duty settled if the sale falls inside the holding period.

Frequently asked questions

Can I remove a co-borrower from a mortgage without their consent?

No. All borrowers on a joint home loan carry joint and several liability, meaning the bank can pursue any of you for the full outstanding amount, and the bank will not release one party from that liability without the exiting borrower's consent as part of a refinancing or loan discharge process. Any removal has to go through a formal restructuring that all parties, and the bank, agree to.

Does removing a co-borrower from the loan also remove them from the property title?

No, these are two separate legal steps. The mortgage loan is a liability between the borrowers and the bank, while the property title reflects ownership. Exiting the loan through refinancing does not by itself transfer the exiting party's ownership share, a separate conveyancing transfer, with its own stamp duty, is required to move the title into the remaining owner's sole name.

What if the remaining borrower cannot qualify for the loan alone?

If the remaining borrower's standalone income fails the Total Debt Servicing Ratio test on the outstanding loan amount, the bank will not approve a solo refinance. The realistic options at that point are bringing in a new co-borrower to replace the exiting one, negotiating a partial paydown to shrink the loan enough to clear TDSR alone, or selling the property and splitting the proceeds.

Is this different from spousal decoupling for stamp duty purposes?

Yes. Spousal decoupling is a specific planning strategy between married couples, often used to free one spouse to buy a second property without Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty, and it can carry particular remission treatment. An unmarried co-buyer exit, between friends, siblings, or an unmarried couple separating, does not carry any spousal exemption, and full Buyer's Stamp Duty applies on the value of the share being transferred, with Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty potentially applying too depending on the remaining owner's existing property count.
