The handover checklist every seller should complete

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 13 July 2026

Quick answer: Completion day legally transfers title through your lawyers, but the physical handover is your own responsibility. On or before completion, take final utility meter readings and close or transfer your accounts, gather every key and access device including any duplicates, collect warranty cards and manuals for included fittings, notify your MCST or town council of the change in ownership, and leave the unit in the condition promised in the Sale and Purchase Agreement. None of this is legally complex, but forgetting a step creates friction with the buyer at the worst possible moment, right after the deal has closed.

Most sellers pour their attention into getting the price right and surviving negotiation, then treat completion day as a formality, a signature and a set of keys. That is a mistake. The legal transfer is handled by your lawyer, but the physical handover, the part that actually determines whether the buyer's first weeks in the property are smooth or irritating, is entirely on you. I have seen deals that closed cleanly on paper turn awkward because a seller forgot to close a utility account or handed over three of four key sets. None of it is expensive to fix. All of it is avoidable with a checklist.

What completion day legally does, and what it does not do

On completion, your lawyer receives the balance purchase price, the legal title transfers to the buyer, and the transaction is recorded with the Singapore Land Authority for private property or processed through HDB's resale system for a flat. That legal machinery runs largely independent of you once your lawyer has the necessary instructions and documents. What it does not do automatically is hand over the physical property in good order. Keys, meters, fittings and notifications are practical steps that sit outside the legal transfer and are easy to overlook precisely because they feel secondary to it.

Utility meters and account closure

Arrange final meter readings for electricity, water and gas ahead of the completion date, and instruct your utility provider to close or transfer the account effective from that date. Getting the timing right matters on both sides: close too early and you risk being without utilities before you have actually vacated, close too late and you are paying for the buyer's usage after ownership has changed hands. Most sellers align the account closure date directly with the legal completion date agreed in the Sale and Purchase Agreement, and confirm the reading with the buyer or their agent so there is no dispute afterward.

Keys and access devices, every single set

This sounds obvious and is still the single most common handover complaint I hear from buyers. Gather every key, front door, gate, letterbox, storeroom, and every access device, estate fobs, car park remotes, resident cards, including any duplicates you or family members made over the years. A buyer who discovers weeks later that a spare key is still floating around is understandably uneasy about it. Make a written list of what you are handing over and have the buyer or their agent acknowledge receipt at handover.

Quick handover inventory

  • All door keys, including gates, storerooms and letterboxes, plus any duplicates.
  • Estate access cards, car park remotes and intercom fobs.
  • Final utility meter readings, with the account closure or transfer confirmed.
  • Warranty cards, manuals and service records for any fittings included in the sale.
  • Written notice to your MCST or town council of the change in ownership.
  • A final walkthrough confirming the unit matches the condition agreed in the Sale and Purchase Agreement.

Fittings, appliances and their warranties

If air conditioning units, water heaters, kitchen appliances or other fittings are included in the sale, as is standard for most resale transactions, gather their warranty cards, purchase receipts and any service history and hand them over at completion. Some manufacturer warranties transfer automatically to a new owner, others require the manufacturer or service provider to be notified of the change, and a few are non transferable altogether. Check each item's terms ahead of time rather than assuming, so you can tell the buyer clearly what is and is not still covered.

Notifying your MCST or town council

For private property, your Management Corporation Strata Title council needs to be informed of the change in ownership so maintenance fees, access cards and estate correspondence transfer correctly to the new owner. For HDB flats, notify your town council so service and conservancy charges are billed to the new owner from the handover date, and any resident passes or access arrangements are reissued in the buyer's name. Skipping this step is a common source of post sale confusion, bills or notices still arriving in the seller's name months after the sale has closed.

Do the final walkthrough with intent, not habit. Before handing over keys, walk the unit against what the Sale and Purchase Agreement actually promised, condition, inclusions and any agreed repairs. This is your last chance to catch a mismatch before it becomes the buyer's problem, and potentially your liability, after completion.

Coordinating with your lawyer on timing

Confirm with your conveyancing lawyer exactly when completion funds have been received before you release keys to the buyer or their agent. Physical handover should follow confirmed legal completion, not anticipate it. If your sale and your own onward purchase are happening close together, this sequencing becomes even more important, since you do not want to hand over one property before funds for your next purchase have cleared. My guide on what delays property completion covers the timing pitfalls that most often trip up sellers juggling both sides of a move.

A simple sequence for the final week

  1. One week before completion: confirm meter reading arrangements, gather all keys and access devices, and locate warranty documents for included fittings.
  2. A few days before: notify your MCST or town council of the change in ownership, and confirm the utility account closure date.
  3. On completion day: confirm with your lawyer that funds have been received, take final meter readings, do the walkthrough, and hand over keys with a written inventory acknowledged by the buyer.
  4. After completion: keep copies of the handover inventory and meter readings for your own records in case any question arises later.

Frequently asked questions

What should a seller do on completion day in Singapore?

On completion day, the seller hands over all sets of keys and access devices, takes final utility meter readings, ensures the unit is left in the agreed condition, and confirms with their lawyer that completion funds have been received before releasing keys. The lawyer handles the legal transfer of title, but the physical handover, keys, meters and condition, is the seller's own responsibility to get right.

Do I need to close my utility accounts before handover?

Yes. Arrange final meter readings and close or transfer your electricity, water and gas accounts effective from the completion date, so you are not billed for usage after you no longer own the property and the buyer is not left without utilities on move in. Align the closing date with the legal completion date.

What happens to fittings and appliance warranties when I sell?

If fittings such as air conditioners, water heaters or built in appliances are included in the sale, gather the warranty cards, receipts and any service records and hand them to the buyer at completion. Some warranties transfer to a new owner automatically, others require notifying the manufacturer, so check the terms in advance rather than assuming.

Do I need to notify my MCST or town council when I sell?

Yes. For private property, notify the Management Corporation Strata Title council so the new owner is registered for maintenance fees, access cards and estate correspondence. For HDB flats, notify the town council so service and conservancy charges transfer to the new owner from the handover date.

What if I forget something during handover and the buyer complains after?

Minor omissions, a missed key or a warranty card, can usually be resolved informally between seller and buyer or through the respective lawyers shortly after completion. More substantial disputes, such as the condition of the unit differing from what was agreed, are better addressed through your conveyancing lawyer with reference to the Sale and Purchase Agreement.
