Choosing an HDB block with good feng shui

By Winfred Quek · 10 minute read · Published 16 August 2026

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · Published 16 August 2026

Quick answer: With an HDB purchase, the block and the unit's position within it are fixed and cannot be renovated away, so this is where feng shui minded buyers spend the most attention. Facing, floor level and what surrounds the block matter more here than almost anywhere else in the buying process. Practitioners generally favour a block that is not facing directly into a T junction road, a unit that does not face a lift, staircase or rubbish chute head on, and surroundings with some greenery rather than a close up wall of another block. Floor number preferences like avoiding four and favouring eight are widespread in Singapore but are a cultural homophone habit rather than classical doctrine, worth knowing even if you do not personally follow it.

Unlike a condo unit, which you can often renovate to fix a layout issue, an HDB block's orientation, floor stack and surroundings are permanent. You are buying into whatever the block already is. That makes this stage of a search worth slowing down for, whether or not you personally hold any of these beliefs, because the buyer after you might.

Facing and what surrounds the block

Start with what the block as a whole faces. A block set with open space, a park, a school field or a low rise structure in front of it is generally read favourably, since there is room for qi, and light, to gather before reaching the building. A block facing directly into a busy main road, and especially one sitting right at a T junction where a road ends and points straight at the building, is one of the more consistently flagged concerns across schools, since the road is seen as channelling fast moving qi straight at the block rather than letting it flow past.

Also look at how close the next block sits and whether your chosen stack faces that block's blank wall at close range. This blocks natural light and airflow regardless of what you believe about qi, which is exactly why it shows up as a concern in both feng shui terms and plain practical ones.

Floor selection: what actually drives the preferences

Floor number preferences in Singapore are mostly a homophone habit rather than a classical school teaching. In Cantonese and several related dialects, the word for four sounds close to the word for death, which is why some private developments skip or relabel the fourth, fourteenth and twenty fourth floors outright, and why floors like eight and its multiples, which sound close to prosperity, tend to be more sought after and sometimes priced at a premium by sellers who know their buyer pool. HDB blocks number their floors straight through without skipping any, so buyers who mind simply rule out the affected levels and unit numbers themselves rather than finding them already relabelled. This is cultural and linguistic, not something written into Form School or Compass School texts, but it is common enough in Singapore that it shapes real demand.

Beyond numerology, floor level has a more classical angle too: very low floors close to ground level are sometimes read as receiving less refined qi, especially if surrounded by other blocks, while very high floors in some schools are considered to be too detached from the ground's supporting qi. Most practitioners treat this as a soft preference for a comfortable middle range rather than a hard rule, and it varies by individual chart and by school.

Where the unit sits within the block

Two units on the same floor of the same block can have very different readings depending on where the front door sits. A unit whose main door opens directly opposite a lift or a staircase is commonly avoided, since qi is thought to rush out of the home into the shaft or stairwell rather than settling. The same logic applies to a door sitting directly next to a rubbish chute, which carries the added practical downside of odour and pest exposure regardless of belief.

End units and corner units get more light and, often, two facing directions instead of one, which is generally favourable, but check what those two facing directions actually point toward before assuming it is automatically better. An end unit that trades a shared wall for a direct view into a neighbouring block's kitchen exhaust or a sharp building corner has swapped one issue for another.

What is nearby, beyond the block itself

Some buyers extend the same thinking to what is near the estate altogether, not just the block: proximity to a temple, a funeral parlour, a hospital, or a cemetery are common enough concerns that they deserve their own treatment, which we cover fully in buying near temples, hospitals and cemeteries in Singapore. Playgrounds and schools, by contrast, are generally read as bringing lively, yang energy to a block and are seen positively by most practitioners and most parents alike, which is a rare case of feng shui preference and practical preference lining up completely.

Markets and hawker centres nearby are generally read as another source of lively yang energy, alongside schools and playgrounds, and most practitioners see nothing negative about ordinary community bustle near a block. The concerns in this guide are specific to sites tied to death, mourning or illness, not to noise or crowds in general; a busy hawker centre a short walk away is a different category entirely from a funeral parlour in direct view, even though both involve more foot traffic than a quiet residential corner.

Mature versus non mature estates

In mature estates, blocks tend to sit closer together and street layouts are often older and less predictable than the more deliberately planned road grids in newer towns, which makes T junction facing and close up block to block facing somewhat more common to encounter and worth checking carefully. In non mature estates, blocks are frequently laid out with wider spacing and more greenery by design, which tends to work in feng shui minded buyers' favour on the surroundings front, though newer estates can still cluster blocks tightly around a single access road, so this is a tendency rather than a guarantee either way. Either estate type can turn up a block with excellent or difficult feng shui; the estate's age is a loose signal at best, and checking the specific block still matters more than any general rule about mature versus non mature towns.

How to check all this before you even book a viewing

HDB's map service and any satellite map let you see a block's orientation, what it faces, and how close neighbouring blocks sit, all before you contact an agent. Cross reference this against the floor plan reading covered in our floor plan guide, and you can shortlist or rule out an entire block before spending a Saturday on viewings. Walking the block yourself at different times of day, particularly evening when void deck and common area activity is highest, also tells you more than any map.

Do not let numerology override everything else. A buyer who rules out an otherwise excellent unit purely because of a floor number, while ignoring a genuinely difficult T junction facing two floors down, has the priorities backwards by most classical teaching. Numerology preferences are real and widespread in Singapore, but they sit alongside facing, surroundings and layout, not above them.

HDB block selection checklist

  • Check what the block as a whole faces: open space, a road, or another block
  • Check whether the block or your stack sits directly at a T junction
  • Check how close the next block is and whether your unit faces its blank wall
  • Decide your own position on floor number preferences before you start shortlisting
  • Check whether the unit's main door faces a lift, staircase or rubbish chute directly
  • Check what an end or corner unit's two facing directions actually point toward
  • Check what the void deck below is typically used for
  • Note any temple, funeral parlour, hospital or cemetery within view or close proximity
  • Check for nearby playgrounds, parks or schools as a positive surrounding factor
  • Walk the block in person at a different time from your scheduled viewing

Frequently asked questions

Why do some buyers avoid the 4th floor or units ending in 4?

It is a homophone belief rather than classical school doctrine: in Cantonese and several other Chinese dialects, the word for four sounds close to the word for die, so some buyers avoid floors and unit numbers containing four out of caution rather than because any classical text singles the number out. Some private developments skip or relabel the fourth floor outright for exactly this reason, while HDB blocks number floors straight through and leave buyers to rule out the level themselves, and floors like eight, which sounds close to prosper in Cantonese, are often more sought after.

Does a corner unit have better or worse feng shui?

Both, depending on what you weight. Corner units usually get more natural light and two facing directions instead of one, which most schools view favourably. The tradeoff is more exposed edges and, in some layouts, a higher chance of facing a sharp corner of an adjacent block or a road bend directly, which is what practitioners call a poison arrow. Check the actual surroundings before assuming a corner unit is automatically better.

Should I avoid a block near a void deck?

Not automatically, but it is worth checking what the void deck below is actually used for. Void decks in Singapore are still occasionally used for wakes and other funeral related gatherings, and some buyers prefer not to live directly above a void deck that sees this kind of use regularly, particularly on the lowest floors. A void deck used mainly as a playground or community space does not carry the same concern for most buyers.

Does facing a main road always hurt a unit's feng shui?

Not always; a road running past the block is normal and unavoidable in most Singapore towns. The specific concern is a unit facing directly into where a road ends or bends sharply toward the block, commonly called a T junction, since the road is seen as sending fast moving qi straight at the unit rather than flowing past it. A block set back from the road, or a unit that does not face the T junction directly, does not carry the same concern.

How much does surrounding greenery matter?

Meaningfully, on both counts. Surrounding greenery is generally viewed favourably as gentle, supportive qi and also happens to improve the view and reduce noise, which is why it shows up as a preference for practical buyers too. Facing directly into another block at close range, sometimes described as a wall of qi, is the more commonly flagged negative, since it blocks light and air along with whatever else a practitioner might read into it.
