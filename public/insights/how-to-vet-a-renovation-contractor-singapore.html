How to vet a renovation contractor: a homeowner's checklist

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 13 July 2026

Quick answer: Vetting a renovation contractor comes down to four checks: business registration and trade credentials such as CaseTrust or BCA, real completed projects you can view or verify with past clients, a proper itemised contract rather than a single lump sum quote, and a payment schedule tied to milestones instead of a large upfront deposit. None of these checks predicts the design outcome, but together they filter out the contractors most likely to disappear mid job, overcharge on variations, or leave defects unresolved. Spend the time here before signing, not after something goes wrong.

Facts verified: 13 July 2026 · Regulatory frameworks referenced are general and subject to change · Sources attributed below

Renovation is the part of a property purchase where buyers, having spent months on financing and legal due diligence, often make the fastest and least examined decision of the whole process: who gets to gut and rebuild the inside of their home. I am not a renovation specialist, but I sit with clients through this stage constantly, and the pattern I see is consistent. The disputes that end up in Small Claims Tribunal almost always trace back to a vetting step that was skipped at the start, not bad luck during the job itself.

Start with business registration and trade credentials

Every legitimate renovation firm should have a registered business entity, which you can check on ACRA's public register. That alone tells you very little beyond the fact the company exists, so layer on trade specific credentials. CaseTrust accreditation, held by many established renovation and interior design firms, signals the firm has agreed to standards around contract terms, deposit handling and a structured complaint process. If the scope of work touches structural elements, hacking of load bearing walls, or general building work, check whether the contractor is registered with the Building and Construction Authority for the relevant category of work.

For HDB flats specifically, HDB maintains a list of registered renovation contractors, and any renovation requiring an HDB permit, which covers most non cosmetic work, must be carried out by a contractor on that list. This is not optional paperwork; using an unregistered contractor for permit required work can leave you personally liable if HDB flags the work during an inspection.

Verify the portfolio against real, contactable clients

A polished portfolio of rendered images or stock photography proves nothing about a contractor's actual workmanship. Ask to see completed projects, ideally ones you can visit or at minimum photograph in detail, and ask for at least two past clients you can contact directly, not just testimonials curated on their website. A contractor confident in their work will not hesitate to connect you. One who becomes evasive at this request is telling you something before the job has even started.

Where possible, ask past clients specifically about how the contractor handled problems, not just whether they liked the final result. Every renovation hits some friction, a delayed material, a site condition that changes the plan, an unexpected cost. How a contractor communicates and resolves that friction tells you far more about the working relationship than the finished photos ever will.

Insist on a proper, itemised contract

A single lump sum quote with no breakdown is the single biggest structural weakness in most renovation disputes I have seen, because it leaves no reference point to dispute overcharging, material substitution, or scope creep. A proper contract should itemise the scope of work by area, specify materials and brands where relevant, particularly for items like flooring, sanitary fittings and kitchen carpentry, and set out a payment schedule tied to defined milestones rather than a large sum paid upfront before work begins.

Read the payment schedule as carefully as the quotation

A contractor asking for full payment before starting work, or a very large deposit disproportionate to the early stage of the job, is a pattern worth taking seriously. A more balanced schedule ties payment to milestones you can physically verify, for example a portion on commencement, a portion on completion of hacking and electrical rough in, a portion on carpentry completion, and a final portion on handover after defects are checked. This structure gives you real leverage throughout the job, because withholding the next payment is your main tool if something is not delivered as agreed.

If you are financing part of the renovation, my guide on topping up your mortgage for renovation covers how that financing interacts with a milestone based payment schedule, since a poorly timed loan drawdown can put you in a weak negotiating position with the contractor.

Spot the red flags before you sign

  • A quotation far below the others with no clear explanation, often a sign of corner cutting on materials or an intention to raise the price through variations later.
  • Vague line items such as one lump figure for all carpentry, with no material, brand or dimension specified.
  • Reluctance to provide a written contract, or pressure to proceed on a verbal agreement and a deposit.
  • No verifiable past projects you can see in person or confirm with a real client.
  • Pressure tactics around limited time offers or discounts that expire if you do not sign immediately.

None of these signs are proof of bad intent on their own, but two or more together should slow you down. A short pause to verify credentials costs you a week. A dispute over unfinished or defective work can cost you months and the renovation budget you had already spent.

What to do once work begins

Vetting does not end at signature. Keep a simple written record, even just messages, of every agreed variation and its price before the work happens, not after. Take photographs at each milestone before the next stage covers it up, particularly electrical and plumbing work that gets hidden behind walls and flooring. If a dispute does arise despite your vetting, the escalation path, from direct negotiation through CASE mediation to Small Claims Tribunal, is covered separately in my guide on what to do about bad renovation contractor work.

Frequently asked questions

How do I check if a renovation contractor is legitimate in Singapore?

Check their business registration on ACRA, look for CaseTrust accreditation or BCA registration where relevant to the scope, and confirm HDB registered renovation contractor status for any HDB permit work. Verify their portfolio against real completed projects and speak to at least two past clients directly.

What should be in a renovation contract before I sign?

An itemised scope of work, specified materials and brands, a payment schedule tied to milestones rather than a large upfront sum, a defined timeline with completion date, a defects rectification clause with a warranty period, and a written variation order process for scope changes.

What are red flags in a renovation quotation?

A quotation significantly lower than others with no explanation, vague line items with no material specification, a request for full payment upfront, no fixed timeline, reluctance to provide a written contract, and no verifiable past projects or contactable references.

Should I use CaseTrust accredited renovation firms only?

CaseTrust accreditation is a useful filter that indicates certain standards around contracts, deposits and complaint handling, and it gives access to a structured mediation path. It is not the only test though, and plenty of smaller unaccredited contractors also produce good work, so treat it as one input among several.

How much deposit should I pay a renovation contractor upfront?

There is no fixed legal figure, but a disproportionately large upfront deposit before any work begins is a common red flag. A more balanced approach ties payment to verifiable milestones through the job, which limits your exposure and gives you leverage if the work does not proceed as agreed.
