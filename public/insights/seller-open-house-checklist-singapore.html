What to do before an open house: the seller's viewing day checklist

By Winfred Quek · 7 minute read · Published 19 August 2026

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · Published 19 August 2026

Quick answer: An open house or viewing block is won or lost in the final 24 hours, not the weeks before it. The night before, do a final declutter pass and check every light bulb and tap. The morning of, air the unit out, set the temperature, and clear away anything personal that made it back onto a surface overnight. Move pets somewhere else for the appointment, and put valuables and personal documents out of sight rather than merely out of reach. If the unit is tenanted, coordinate access under your tenancy agreement well ahead of time, do not assume you can simply show up. During the viewing itself, let your agent run the conversation, and afterward, ask for feedback while it is still fresh rather than waiting to see if an offer arrives.

If you have already handled the broader work of getting a property ready to list, decluttering room by room, fixing the small things, a proper clean, this guide picks up where that leaves off. It covers the specific window around an open house or a block of back to back viewings, the night before, the morning of, and the hour itself, when the goal is no longer to fix anything but simply to protect the work you already put in.

The night before

Do a final walkthrough with fresh eyes, ideally at the same time of day the viewing is scheduled, so you see the unit the way a buyer will. Check every light bulb, a single dead bulb reads as neglect even in an otherwise spotless room. Wipe down surfaces that collect clutter fast, kitchen counters, the dining table, bedside tables, since these tend to refill within a day of a proper clean. Lay out or pack away anything you will need for the morning so you are not searching for keys or shoes while buyers are already arriving.

The morning of

Air the unit out well before the first appointment, especially if you cooked the night before, and set the temperature so the space feels comfortable the moment someone walks in rather than needing ten minutes to cool down or warm up. Open curtains for a daytime viewing and turn on lights for anything scheduled toward evening. Clear the surfaces one more time, mail, dishes and laundry have a way of reappearing overnight, and a five minute reset before you leave the unit makes a real difference to the first impression.

Managing pets and valuables

Move pets out of the unit for the viewing if you possibly can, a friend, a pet hotel, or a walk timed around the appointment all work. Not every buyer is comfortable around animals, and an anxious pet becomes the story of the viewing instead of the property itself. Put valuables, jewellery, cash, and any documents with personal or financial information, out of sight rather than merely out of reach. A viewing brings people you do not know into your home, and tidying this away is simple prevention, not a judgement on anyone attending.

If the unit is tenanted

Coordinate access with your tenant well ahead of the viewing rather than assuming you can arrange entry on short notice. Check your tenancy agreement for any notice period that applies to landlord access, and agree on a specific time window with your tenant rather than a general heads up. Buyers viewing a tenanted unit are also weighing up a very different purchase to a vacant one, so it helps to think through, before the first viewing, how you want to present the tenancy itself. Our guide on buying property with an existing tenant covers what a buyer is actually assessing, which is worth understanding from your side of the table too.

During the viewing

Let your agent run the conversation. Buyers tend to ask more candid questions, and speak more openly about what they actually think, when the seller is not standing in the room listening to every word. If you are home, keep a low profile, a friendly greeting is enough, and resist the urge to narrate every feature as buyers walk through on their own. Keep a simple record of who viewed and when, your agent will usually handle this, since it makes following up afterward far easier than trying to remember names from memory.

After the viewing

Ask for feedback while it is still fresh, ideally the same day. Specific, honest feedback, on price, on presentation, on the unit itself, is more useful than silence, even when it is not what you want to hear. If the same comment comes up across several viewings, that is worth acting on before the next round rather than dismissing it as one opinion.

Reset the unit properly afterward too, rather than leaving it in showing condition indefinitely. Put valuables and documents back where they normally live once the viewing is over, and treat each appointment as a fresh setup rather than assuming the previous one will still be in order for the next buyer who comes through later in the week.

One viewing at a time, or several together

Both approaches are common, and which one suits you depends on your unit, your schedule and your agent's marketing strategy. Grouping viewings into a single block can create a sense of activity that individual appointments do not, while spacing them out gives each buyer more time and privacy to properly consider the space. Frequently asked questions

How long before a viewing should I start preparing?

The presentation work, decluttering, repairs, cleaning, should already be done by the time a viewing is scheduled. This checklist covers the final stretch, the night before and the morning of, when the goal shifts from fixing things to simply making sure nothing undoes the work you already put in.

Should I be home during the viewing?

It generally works better if you step out and let your agent run the viewing, since buyers tend to speak more openly and picture themselves in the space without the seller standing in the room. If you do stay, keep a low profile and let your agent lead the conversation.

What do I do about pets during a viewing?

Move pets out of the unit for the viewing if you can, a friend, a pet hotel, or simply a walk timed around the appointment. Not every buyer is comfortable around animals, and a barking dog or a curious cat becomes the story of the viewing instead of the property.

My unit is tenanted, how much notice do I need to give before a viewing?

Check your tenancy agreement first, most set out a minimum notice period for access, and reasonable practice is to coordinate a specific time with your tenant rather than showing up unannounced. Our guide on buying property with an existing tenant covers the position from the buyer's side, which is worth understanding before you schedule anything.

Should I schedule one viewing at a time or several back to back?

Both approaches work, and the right one depends on your unit and your agent's strategy. Back to back viewings can create a sense of activity and urgency, while individual appointments let each buyer take their time.
