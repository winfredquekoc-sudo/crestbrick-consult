Does feng shui affect Singapore property value

By Winfred Quek · 10 minute read · Published 14 August 2026

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · Published 14 August 2026

Quick answer: Feng shui is not going to make or break a Singapore transaction on its own, but it is a genuine factor in how a meaningful slice of buyers and tenants filter listings, and that shows up in demand. Practitioners hold that layout, facing and surroundings affect the flow of qi through a home, and enough people act on some version of that belief that agents see it in enquiry patterns, viewing turnout, and how quickly certain units move relative to comparable neighbours. Treat it as one more factor that widens or narrows your buyer pool, alongside layout efficiency, floor level and location. It rarely overrides fundamentals like district, size and condition, but at the margin, on a unit that is otherwise comparable to what is next door, it can matter.

Ask five Singapore property agents whether feng shui affects price and you will likely get five different answers, most some version of "it depends who is buying." That is actually the most honest answer available. This is not a belief every buyer holds, and it will never appear as a line item on a bank valuation. But it is also not nothing, and pretending it does not exist ignores a real slice of the buyer pool you may be selling to, or the tenant pool you may be renting to.

Why feng shui shows up in Singapore pricing at all

The mechanism is simple demand economics, not anything mystical. Singapore's resident population includes a large number of Chinese families, many of whom follow feng shui principles to varying degrees, from a light touch on entrance clutter to a full practitioner consultation before signing an option to purchase. Add in tenants and buyers from mainland China, Hong Kong and Taiwan, where classical feng shui teaching is often more deeply embedded in everyday property decisions, and you get a segment of the market that actively screens listings for certain features and actively avoids others.

When a slice of the buyer pool self selects out of a listing, that listing has fewer people bidding for it. Fewer bidders usually means a longer time on market or a seller who has to be more flexible on price to attract the buyers who remain. None of this requires anyone to believe feng shui works. It only requires enough people to act as if it does, which agents will tell you happens every week across HDB resale and private viewings.

What practitioners look at first

Classical feng shui evaluates a home on two broad layers. The first is Form School, sometimes called luan tou, which looks at the physical landform and surroundings: what the unit faces, what is behind it for support, whether the entrance has a clear and gathering space in front of it, and whether anything sharp or straight points at the door. The second is Compass School, or li qi, which layers direction and time on top of the physical form, including the bagua map and, for practitioners who go deeper, the flying star chart tied to the building's construction period.

In practice, most buyers never go past a simplified checklist drawn loosely from both schools: does the main door line up straight with the back door or a window, does it face a lift or staircase directly, is there a toilet sitting in the centre of the unit, is the floor plan an irregular shape with an obvious missing corner, and does the unit face straight into a T junction road. We cover how to read these off a floor plan before you even view in our practical floor plan guide.

Where the effect is strongest

The effect tends to be most visible in HDB resale and older private resale, where the buyer is inspecting a fixed, unchangeable block and unit position, and where enough comparable units exist nearby that a buyer can simply choose a similar unit without the flagged issue. Our guide to choosing an HDB block with good feng shui goes through facing, floor and surroundings in detail, since these are exactly the features that are fixed and cannot be renovated away.

It is also strongest at the landed and good class bungalow end of the private market, where the stakes and the price quantum are large enough that engaging a feng shui consultant formally, sometimes alongside the architect, is routine rather than unusual. Buyers here often treat a practitioner's sign off the way they treat a structural survey: not mandatory, but common enough that its absence gets noticed.

Where it is overstated

The effect is weaker, and sometimes negligible, in well built modern developments with regular floor plates, where the developer's architect has already avoided most of the obvious layout problems simply by designing a sensible, efficient home. A rectangular unit with a foyer, a main door that does not line up with anything straight through it, and a stove that is not visible from the entrance will satisfy most feng shui minded buyers without either party ever using the term. Good design and feng shui screening overlap more than either side likes to admit, a point we unpack fully in feng shui myths versus good design.

It is also weaker in tightly held Core Central Region and Rest of Central Region markets, where scarcity, location and school proximity tend to dominate buyer decisions regardless of layout quirks. A buyer who has waited years for a specific address is less likely to walk away over a facing issue than a buyer choosing between a dozen similar HDB blocks in the same town.

How sellers and agents actually respond

In practice this rarely turns into a full renovation. Sellers who are told a unit has a flagged issue usually respond with staging, not construction: decluttering the entrance, repositioning furniture to open up a commanding view of the living room, fixing anything that reads as neglect such as a water stained ceiling or a broken tile, and timing viewings so the unit is well lit. Our guide to feng shui renovations before you sell covers this in more depth, walking through what is worth doing and what is not before you spend on anything structural.

A flagged layout is often a genuinely weaker layout, not only a matter of belief. A missing corner that concerns a feng shui minded buyer is often also a space efficiency problem in square footage terms. A main door that lines up straight through to a window can also mean less privacy and a colder draft path. Do not dismiss every flagged issue as pure belief; some of it doubles as a plain, practical design complaint.

Does this affect rental as much as sale

Rental demand responds to the same beliefs, though usually with less at stake for the tenant than for an owner making a long term purchase. A tenant signing a one or two year lease is generally more willing to overlook a flagged layout issue than a buyer committing to a purchase, since the exposure is shorter and easier to walk away from at renewal. That said, landlords who ignore this entirely can still see it show up as a smaller pool of enquiries for an otherwise well priced unit, particularly for tenant profiles where feng shui consideration tends to run deeper. Our feng shui for rental units guide, written for landlords, covers this from the other side of the transaction in full.

A quick checklist before you price or view a unit

  • Does the main door line up straight with a back door or a window across the unit
  • Does the main door face a lift, staircase or rubbish chute directly
  • Is there a toilet sitting in the centre of the unit, sometimes called the tai chi point
  • Is the unit an irregular shape with an obvious missing corner
  • Does the unit face directly into a T junction road or a flyover
  • Is the block or building facing another building's blank wall at close range
  • Is the main bedroom directly above or below a kitchen stove, relevant mainly in maisonettes and landed homes
  • Does the stove face the main door in a straight line
  • Is there a mirror directly opposite the main door or facing a bed
  • Would a feng shui minded buyer likely want a practitioner's visit to feel comfortable, or would basic staging probably be enough

Frequently asked questions

Does feng shui really affect Singapore property prices?

It can, but usually at the margin rather than as the main driver. Practitioners hold that layout, facing and surroundings affect the flow of qi through a home, and a meaningful slice of Singapore buyers and tenants screen for this. Agents commonly see it show up in enquiry patterns and viewing turnout rather than as a line item on a valuation. Location, size, condition and asking price still do most of the work.

Which flaws do feng shui minded buyers avoid most?

The most commonly avoided issues include a main door that lines up straight with a back door or window, a main door facing a lift or staircase directly, a toilet positioned in the centre of the unit, an irregular shape with a missing corner, and a unit facing directly into a T junction road. These come up repeatedly across HDB and private resale viewings.

Should I fix feng shui issues before selling?

Start with low cost, reversible changes: decluttering the entry, fixing water leaks promptly, improving lighting, and adjusting furniture placement. Structural changes purely to chase a feng shui outcome are rarely worth the cost or approval hassle ahead of a resale sale. Our feng shui renovations before you sell guide covers this in more detail.

Do new launch condos have feng shui considerations too?

Yes. Site layout, stack position, facing, and proximity to a T junction or another block's blank wall apply to new launches just as they do to resale property. The difference is that buyers are working from a site plan and floor plan rather than a physical walk through, so reading the paperwork carefully matters more.

Is feng shui more important for HDB or private property?

Neither market is exempt, but the emphasis differs. HDB resale buyers often weigh block facing, floor level and surroundings heavily because the estate and block are fixed and highly visible. Private buyers, especially at the landed and good class bungalow end, more often engage a practitioner formally as part of due diligence, given the higher stakes and the fact that landed layouts can sometimes be altered.
