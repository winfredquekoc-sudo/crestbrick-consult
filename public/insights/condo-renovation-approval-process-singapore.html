Getting your condo renovation approved by the MCST

By Winfred Quek · 8 minute read · Published 13 July 2026

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 13 July 2026

Quick answer: Before any Singapore condo renovation begins, most Management Corporation Strata Titles require you to submit a renovation permit application, pay a refundable contractor deposit, and confine hacking and noisy works to the hours and areas set out in the development's house rules. The specific forms, fees and time windows differ by development because each MCST writes its own rules under the Building Maintenance and Strata Management Act, but the shape of the process is the same everywhere: submit plans, get approval, lodge the deposit, brief your contractor, and expect an inspection at the end. Skip the process and you risk a stop work order, a forfeited deposit, or a bill for damage to common property.

Almost every renovation dispute I hear about from clients starts the same way: an owner assumed that because the unit is theirs, the renovation is entirely their own business. It rarely is. In a strata development, your unit sits inside a building that hundreds of other owners share an interest in, and the pipes, walls, waterproofing and structure you might touch during a renovation often belong to, or affect, everyone else. That is why the Management Corporation Strata Title, or MCST, sits between you and your contractor before a single tile gets hacked. Understanding that process properly, rather than treating it as a formality to route around, is what keeps a renovation on schedule instead of turning into a dispute.

Why the MCST has a say in your renovation

The legal basis for MCST oversight sits in the Building Maintenance and Strata Management Act, which gives the management corporation responsibility for common property and the authority to make house rules governing how owners use and alter their units where those alterations could affect the building, other units, or shared services. In practice, this means the MCST council adopts a renovation policy, often called house rules or additions and alterations guidelines, that every owner and their contractor must follow.

The reasoning is straightforforward once you see the building as a shared structure rather than a stack of separate homes. Hacking a wall in your unit can be structural to the unit above or below. Rerouting a pipe can affect the riser that serves six other units on your stack. Noise from hacking work travels through concrete far more than most owners expect. The MCST's job is to make sure one owner's renovation does not become another owner's problem, and the permit process is the mechanism for checking that before, not after, the work starts.

The permit application step by step

The exact form varies by development, but the sequence is broadly consistent across Singapore condos. First, you submit a renovation application to the MCST or its managing agent, typically including your intended scope of works, a rough schedule, and your contractor's details and licence information. Second, for anything involving hacking, plumbing or electrical changes, the MCST usually asks for drawings or a written description detailed enough to assess whether structural or common property elements are involved. Third, the council or the managing agent reviews the application and either approves it, approves it with conditions, or asks for a professional engineer's endorsement if structural elements are involved.

Once approved, you will usually be asked to sign an acknowledgement that you and your contractor accept the house rules, lodge the contractor deposit, and in some developments display an approval notice or permit near your unit's entrance during the works. Only after this full sequence is complete should hacking or noisy work actually begin. Starting before written approval is issued is one of the most common and most avoidable mistakes owners make, often because a contractor is eager to start and assures the owner that approval is a formality.

The contractor deposit: what it covers and how you get it back

Nearly every MCST requires a refundable deposit before renovation work starts. The amount is set individually by each development, scaled loosely to the size and risk of the works planned, so there is genuinely no fixed figure that applies across Singapore condos. What is consistent is the purpose: the deposit exists to cover damage to lifts, common corridors, lobbies, and any shared property caused during the delivery of materials or the works themselves, and to give the MCST leverage to enforce the house rules while the renovation is under way.

Getting the deposit back typically depends on a post renovation inspection. The managing agent or a council member checks common areas along the route your contractor used, confirms there are no unresolved complaints from neighbours, and verifies the completed works match what was approved. If everything is in order, the deposit, or the remaining balance after any deductions, is returned within a set period after the inspection. If damage is found, repair costs are deducted first, which is exactly why a careful owner walks the common corridors with their contractor before work starts and again before requesting the refund, so any pre existing marks are documented and not wrongly attributed to the renovation.

What you can and cannot hack

Owners generally have real latitude over non structural, purely internal elements: tiles, flooring finishes, non load bearing partition walls, built in furniture, and fittings that sit entirely within the unit's own footprint. Where the MCST tightens scrutiny is anything that could touch load bearing walls, structural slabs, the building's waterproofing membrane, or shared risers and pipes that serve multiple units. Bathroom and kitchen hacking gets particular attention because compromised waterproofing is the single most common cause of the leak disputes between units that I cover in a companion guide.

Some developments also restrict changes that alter the external appearance of the unit as seen from outside, such as balcony enclosures, aircon ledge modifications, or window replacements, since these can affect the building's facade uniformity and sometimes trigger separate planning considerations. If your renovation plan involves anything beyond cosmetic finishes, it is worth asking the managing agent directly whether a professional engineer's certification will be required, rather than assuming your contractor's word is sufficient for MCST purposes.

Working hours and noise rules

Renovation noise is consistently the top source of neighbour complaints in strata living, which is why nearly every MCST sets specific working hours for hacking and other noisy work, usually confined to weekday daytime windows and excluding early mornings, evenings, Sundays and public holidays. Quieter finishing work such as painting or furniture installation is often permitted on a somewhat wider schedule. The exact hours differ by development, so confirm them directly with your managing agent rather than assuming a standard citywide rule applies, because none exists.

A practical habit worth adopting: brief your contractor explicitly on the approved hours in writing, not just verbally, and ask them to plan the noisiest phases, typically hacking and drilling, for the earliest part of the approved window each day rather than pushing them late. This reduces the odds of a neighbour complaint reaching the MCST mid project, which can trigger a warning or even a temporary stop work instruction while the complaint is investigated.

What happens if you skip approval or breach the rules

Unapproved work rarely stays hidden. Renovation noise, contractor traffic through common areas, and skip bins in the loading bay are all visible to the MCST and to other residents. Skipping the permit process does not usually go unnoticed, it just delays the point at which it becomes a problem, and by then reversing unauthorised structural changes is far more expensive than getting approval would have been.

If the MCST discovers unapproved works in progress, the usual response is a stop work order, halting the renovation until proper approval is obtained retroactively or the unauthorised changes are reversed. Persistent non compliance can escalate to the Strata Titles Boards or the courts, and any deposit lodged can be forfeited or applied toward reinstatement costs. There is also a longer tail risk: when you eventually sell, a careful buyer's lawyer or the incoming MCST may ask whether past renovations, especially bathroom hacking or structural changes, were properly approved, and an unclear answer can complicate or delay the sale.

Getting through the process smoothly

  1. Read the house rules before you engage a contractor. Request the MCST's renovation guidelines early so your contractor quotes against the actual rules rather than a generic assumption.
  2. Document the common areas before work starts. Photograph the lift, lobby and corridor condition on day one so pre existing marks are not charged against your deposit.
  3. Put working hours in writing to your contractor. Verbal briefings get forgotten under deadline pressure; a written schedule does not.
  4. Budget time for approval, not just for the works. Structural elements that need a professional engineer's sign off can add real time to the timeline, so build that into your renovation schedule from the start.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need MCST approval to renovate my condo unit?

Yes, in almost every Singapore condo development. The MCST adopts house rules under the Building Maintenance and Strata Management Act requiring owners to submit a renovation permit before any work involving hacking, plumbing changes, electrical rewiring or works affecting common property. Even cosmetic work like painting or flooring often needs a simple notification. Always check your development's house rules before booking a contractor.

How much is a typical renovation deposit and when do I get it back?

The amount is set by each MCST and varies by development and by the scale of the works planned, so there is no single figure that applies island wide. It is refundable, typically returned after a post renovation inspection confirms no damage to common property, no unresolved complaints, and full compliance with the approved scope. If damage is found, repair costs are deducted before any balance is returned.

What can I hack in my unit and what needs MCST sign off?

You generally have latitude over internal, non structural finishes such as tiles, non load bearing partition walls, and fittings within your own unit. You almost always need specific sign off, and sometimes a professional engineer's endorsement, for anything touching load bearing walls, structural slabs, the waterproofing membrane, or shared risers and pipes. Bathroom and kitchen hacking is scrutinised closely because it is the most common source of water leak disputes between units.

What are typical renovation working hours in Singapore condos?

Most MCSTs restrict noisy or hacking work to weekdays within a defined daytime window, excluding early mornings, evenings, Sundays and public holidays, though exact hours are set by each development's house rules and should be confirmed before scheduling. Quieter finishing work such as painting may be allowed on a wider schedule. Breaching working hours is one of the most common sources of neighbour complaints.

What happens if I renovate without MCST approval?

The MCST can issue a stop work order, require reinstatement of unauthorised changes at your own cost, withhold or forfeit any deposit, and in persistent cases pursue the matter through the Strata Titles Boards or the courts. Unapproved works also complicate a future sale, since a buyer's lawyer or the incoming MCST may ask for proof that past renovations were properly authorised.
