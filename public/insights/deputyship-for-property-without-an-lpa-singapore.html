What happens to a property decision if there's no LPA

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 13 July 2026

Quick answer: If a property owner loses mental capacity without ever signing a Lasting Power of Attorney, no family member automatically gains authority to manage or sell their property, even a spouse. The family must apply to the Family Justice Courts for a deputyship order, appointing one or more deputies to act on the person's behalf. This process requires a formal medical capacity assessment, a court application, and typically takes considerably longer and costs more than using an already registered LPA, and the court decides who is appointed and what powers they get, rather than the family choosing freely. This guide walks through how the process works, what it costs in time, and how it compares to having planned ahead with an LPA.

I see this scenario more often than families expect, and it usually arrives at the worst possible time: a parent has a stroke, or a diagnosis progresses faster than anyone planned for, and suddenly the family realises no one has legal authority to touch the property that is meant to fund the person's care. There was no Lasting Power of Attorney, and there is no shortcut. This is what actually happens next, and why it is so much harder than the alternative would have been.

The starting point: no one has automatic authority

This is the fact that surprises most families. In Singapore, being someone's spouse, child, or next of kin does not give you automatic legal authority to manage or sell their property once they lose mental capacity. Banks will freeze meaningful access, a conveyancing lawyer will not proceed on a family member's instruction alone, and the Singapore Land Authority will not register a transaction without proof of legal authority. Love and proximity do not substitute for a legal instrument. If there is no registered Lasting Power of Attorney, covered in my LPA and property transactions guide, the only route left is an application to court.

What a deputyship order actually is

A deputyship order is made by the Family Justice Courts under the Mental Capacity Act. It appoints one or more deputies, chosen from among those who apply and whom the court considers suitable, and defines the specific powers those deputies are given over the incapacitated person's property, finances, or personal welfare. Unlike an LPA, where the donor chooses their own donee and largely defines the scope themselves while still capable, a deputyship order is decided entirely by the court, based on what it determines is in the person's best interests.

The court decides, not the family. Even close, well meaning families sometimes disagree on who should be deputy or how a property should be handled. The court's role is to resolve that on the basis of the incapacitated person's best interests, which means the outcome may not match what any one family member expected or wanted going in.

The application process, step by step

  1. Medical capacity assessment. A qualified medical practitioner must formally assess and certify that the person lacks capacity to manage their own property and affairs. This assessment underpins the entire application and cannot be skipped.
  2. Filing the application. The applicant, typically a family member, files an application with the Family Justice Courts, setting out who should be appointed deputy, the property and financial matters involved, and the specific powers being requested.
  3. Notice to interested parties. Other family members and interested parties are generally notified of the application, giving them the opportunity to raise objections or apply to be considered as an alternative deputy.
  4. Court consideration and order. The court reviews the application, may request further information or a hearing, and issues an order defining who is appointed, what powers they hold, and any conditions, such as a requirement to file periodic accounts or seek further court approval for major transactions like selling the family home.
  5. Acting under the order. Once granted, the deputy uses the court order, much as a donee would use a registered LPA, to instruct banks, conveyancing lawyers, and the Singapore Land Authority, but strictly within the powers and conditions the court set.

Why this takes longer and costs more than an LPA

The comparison against an LPA is stark. A registered LPA can be used almost immediately once the medical capacity threshold is crossed, because the donee's authority and scope were already defined and accepted years earlier. A deputyship application starts from zero: the medical assessment, the court filing, the notice period, and the court's own consideration of what is appropriate all take time, generally running to several months at minimum, longer if any party contests the application or the court wants additional safeguards given the size or complexity of the estate.

There are also costs the family bears that an LPA would have avoided entirely: legal fees for the court application, the medical assessment fee, and often ongoing costs if the court requires periodic reporting or supervision of the deputy's decisions. None of this is a reason to panic if your family is in this position now, but it is the clearest possible case for why every property owning family should set up an LPA while everyone is still well, rather than relying on this route as a fallback.

Selling property under a deputyship order

If the family's goal is to sell the property, for example to fund long term care, the deputyship order needs to specifically authorise that. Courts are typically more cautious about authorising the sale of a primary residence than about authorising routine management, such as paying bills or collecting rent, because a sale is irreversible. The court may impose additional conditions on a sale, such as requiring an independent valuation or specifying how proceeds must be held and used for the person's benefit. This is a materially more constrained process than a donee under a well drafted LPA would face for the same sale.

What families in this position should do

  • Get proper legal advice early. A lawyer experienced in Mental Capacity Act applications can shape the request appropriately and avoid delays from an incomplete or poorly scoped application.
  • Agree within the family before filing. Disputes over who should be deputy slow the process considerably. Reaching agreement as a family before the application goes in is worth the effort.
  • Keep other affairs organised while the application proceeds. Bills, property tax, and maintenance still need to be paid during the months the application is pending; plan for that gap.
  • Use this as the trigger to plan for the rest of the family. If this has happened to one parent without an LPA, it is the moment to set up LPAs, wills, and CPF nominations for every other family member while they are still able to.

Frequently asked questions

What is a deputyship order in Singapore?

A deputyship order is a Family Justice Courts order appointing one or more people, called deputies, to make decisions on behalf of someone who has lost mental capacity and did not sign a Lasting Power of Attorney beforehand. For property, the order can grant the deputy authority to manage or sell the person's property, but the scope is set and reviewed by the court, not chosen freely by the family.

How long does it take to get a deputyship order for property in Singapore?

A deputyship application takes considerably longer than using an already registered LPA, generally running to several months from filing to a final order, because it requires a medical capacity assessment, a formal court application, and time for the court to consider the appropriate scope of the deputy's powers. Contested applications, such as disputes between family members over who should be deputy, take longer still.

Can any family member apply to be a deputy?

Any person with a genuine interest in the welfare of the person who has lost capacity can apply, typically a spouse, adult child, or close relative, but the court decides who is appointed and may require more than one deputy or impose conditions such as periodic reporting. Being a spouse or child does not create an automatic right to be appointed deputy.

Is deputyship the same as having an LPA?

No. An LPA is set up in advance by the person while they still have capacity, naming their own chosen donee and defining the scope of authority themselves. Deputyship is a court process that only becomes necessary because no LPA exists, and the court, not the incapacitated person, decides who is appointed and what powers they are given. Deputyship is generally slower, costlier, and less flexible than a properly set up LPA.
