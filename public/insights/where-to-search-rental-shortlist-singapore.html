Where to search and how to build your rental shortlist

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · Published 28 August 2026

Quick answer: Most first time tenants build their shortlist from the major listing portals, then narrow it down using three tests: does it fit the budget from Part 2, does the commute actually work at the time you will really travel, and does it clear your must have list. Key Takeaways

  • • Build your shortlist against the budget ceiling you set in Part 2, not against how a listing feels.
  • • Listing photos are a starting impression, not a fact. Wide angle lenses and staging can flatter a room.
  • • Test your commute at the time you would actually travel, not off peak.

  • • Any request to pay before you have viewed the unit in person is a serious early red flag.

With a budget set from Part 2 of this series, the next step is turning a wide open market into a short list of units actually worth your time to view. This part covers where first time tenants search, how to read a listing without being misled by it, a simple shortlisting method, and the early signals that a listing deserves caution before you go any further.

Where first time tenants actually search

The major listing portals such as PropertyGuru and 99.co carry the bulk of active residential rental listings in Singapore and are the natural starting point for a first search. Alongside the portals, property agents often have access to units that have not been listed publicly yet, and word of mouth, community groups, and company relocation networks account for a meaningful share of rentals as well, particularly for room only arrangements. Using more than one channel widens your shortlist without extra cost.

Whichever channel a lead comes from, verify that the person you are dealing with is who they claim to be. If an agent is involved, they should be CEA registered, and you can check this directly at the CEA Public Register before you rely on anything they tell you about the unit or the landlord.

How to read a listing critically

A listing is marketing, not a survey report, and it pays to read it that way.

  • Photos vs reality. Wide angle lenses make rooms look larger and more regular than they are, and staged furniture can hide an awkward layout or poor natural light. Treat every photo as a starting impression that a viewing will confirm or correct.
  • Listing age. A listing that has been up for an unusually long time relative to similar units nearby is worth a question, not necessarily a red flag, sometimes it simply means the asking rent needs adjusting, sometimes it means something about the unit puts people off at viewing.
  • Floor plan against photos. Where a floor plan is provided, check that the room count and rough proportions match what the photos show. A mismatch is usually just an old floor plan on file, but it is worth confirming before you get attached to a layout that may not be accurate.
  • What is missing. A listing that gives no useful detail on facing, floor level, or furnishing status is not necessarily a problem, but it does mean more of your questions need answering at the viewing itself, covered in Part 4 of this series.

A simple shortlisting method

Run every candidate through the same three tests before you book a viewing.

The budget test. Does the all in monthly cost, rent plus any known recurring charges, sit at or under the ceiling you set in Part 2? If not, it does not make the shortlist regardless of how good it looks.
The commute test. Check the actual journey at the time of day you would really be travelling, typically the morning peak, using the mode of transport you would actually use. A route that looks fine at midday can be a different experience during the rush.
The must have test. Score the unit against the must have versus nice to have list from Part 1. A unit that fails a genuine must have does not belong on the shortlist just because it is otherwise appealing.

Early red flags worth slowing down for

Most listings are exactly what they claim to be, and most agents and landlords are straightforward to deal with. A small number of situations are worth extra caution before you go further.

  • Pressure to pay before viewing. Any request for a deposit, booking fee, or advance payment before you have physically viewed the unit is a serious signal to slow down, not a way to secure a good deal faster.
  • A rent that looks too good. A unit priced well below every comparable listing you have shortlisted nearby, with no obvious explanation, deserves a question before it deserves an offer.
  • Reluctance to verify. If an agent will not confirm their CEA registration, or a landlord will not confirm they actually own or are authorised to rent out the unit, treat that as reason enough to walk away.
Keep this in proportion. The overwhelming majority of Singapore rental listings and the agents behind them are legitimate. These signals exist to help you pause on the small minority that are not, not to make you suspicious of every listing you see.

Winfred's Take

The tenants who move fastest and most safely are the ones who did their shortlisting homework before they ever booked a viewing. If you walk in already knowing your budget ceiling, your must haves, and roughly what comparable units nearby are asking, you can tell within five minutes whether a unit is worth serious consideration. Without that groundwork, every viewing becomes its own negotiation with yourself, and that is when people either overpay or freeze and decide nothing.

Frequently asked questions

Which portals do most tenants use to search for a rental in Singapore?

The major listing portals such as PropertyGuru and 99.co carry the bulk of active rental listings, alongside property agents working their own networks and word of mouth. Most first time tenants use a portal to build the first shortlist, then work with an agent to actually view and negotiate.

How do I tell if listing photos are misleading?

Wide angle lenses make rooms look larger than they are, and staged furniture can hide awkward proportions or a lack of natural light. Treat every photo as a starting impression, not a fact, and verify size, layout, and condition in person before you decide anything.

What counts as a red flag before I even view a unit?

Any pressure to transfer money before you have physically viewed the unit, a rent that sits well below every comparable listing nearby with no explanation, and reluctance from the other side to confirm their identity or the landlord's ownership are all reasons to slow down rather than move faster.

Should I test the commute before committing to a shortlist?

Yes. Test the actual journey at the time of day you would really be travelling, typically the morning peak, rather than trusting an off peak estimate from a map app. A commute that looks fine at midday can be a very different experience at half past eight.

How many units should I shortlist before I start viewing?

Enough to compare properly, commonly a handful that all clear your budget and must have list. A single unit viewed in isolation gives you no basis for judging whether it is actually good value or simply the first thing you saw.
