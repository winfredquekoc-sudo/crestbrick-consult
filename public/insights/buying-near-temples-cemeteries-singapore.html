Buying near temples, hospitals and cemeteries

By Winfred Quek · 10 minute read · Published 22 August 2026

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · Published 22 August 2026

Quick answer: Many practitioners and buyers hold that proximity to sites strongly associated with death or mourning, cemeteries, columbaria and funeral parlours most consistently, carries yin energy best avoided for a primary residence. Hospitals split opinion more evenly. Separate from belief, there are practical concerns that overlap: funeral traffic and wake noise near void decks, incense smoke from temples, and higher visitor traffic around Qing Ming and the Hungry Ghost month. In practice, agents commonly see a smaller buyer pool and sometimes a longer time on market for units with a direct, close up view of such a site, though this varies by distance, obstruction and how particular the buyer pool in that specific area happens to be. It is not universal, and plenty of such units sell well to buyers who are simply not particular.

This is one of the more sensitive topics in Singapore property conversations, precisely because it touches on death and mourning directly rather than abstract layout theory. It deserves a plain, respectful treatment rather than either dismissal or exaggeration, since real buyers hold real views on this, and real sellers need to know what they are working with.

The belief, plainly stated

Many practitioners and a meaningful share of buyers hold that places strongly associated with death, mourning or illness carry what is described as yin energy, in contrast to the yang energy considered more suitable for an active family home. Cemeteries, columbaria and funeral parlours are the sites most consistently raised in this context. Temples are treated differently by most practitioners: a temple itself is often viewed as a place of yang energy, given active worship, but a direct view of specific elements such as the rear of the building, incense burners, or an adjoining ritual area is more commonly flagged than the temple's presence in general.

It is worth being clear that this is presented here as belief, not as an assertion of fact about any supernatural effect. What is factual is that a meaningful number of Singapore buyers and tenants act on this belief when choosing where to live, which is why it belongs in a practical guide rather than being waved away.

The practical concerns that overlap with the belief

Separate from the belief itself, there are ordinary, non spiritual reasons some buyers prefer distance from these sites. Funeral wakes, which in Singapore are still sometimes held at void decks near a deceased person's home, bring noise, lighting and unfamiliar visitor traffic that some residents find disruptive, particularly overnight. Temples can produce incense smoke that drifts toward nearby units depending on wind direction, which some residents find irritating regardless of their views on feng shui. Visitor traffic around cemeteries and columbaria rises sharply around Qing Ming, the spring tomb sweeping period, and during the seventh lunar month, popularly known as the Hungry Ghost month, when many families visit ancestral sites and temples hold additional rites.

Hospitals carry their own practical profile: ambulance sirens, drop off traffic, and round the clock activity that some residents find reassuring for access to care and others find simply noisy, independent of any belief about the hospital itself.

It is also worth distinguishing between a traditional ground cemetery and a columbarium, since Singapore's land constraints mean most current facilities for the deceased are columbaria housing niches for ashes rather than the ground burial cemeteries that were more common in earlier decades, with only a small number of historic cemeteries remaining. Some buyers treat the two the same; others draw a distinction, viewing a columbarium as a quieter, more contained facility than a large open ground cemetery. Ask which type of facility you are actually dealing with rather than assuming, since the practical footprint, visitor patterns and visual presence of the two can differ considerably.

What this does to resale and rental in practice

Agents commonly see a smaller buyer pool for units with a direct, unobstructed view of a cemetery, columbarium or funeral parlour compared with an otherwise identical unit facing another direction. A smaller pool of interested buyers can translate into a longer time on market or a seller who has more room to negotiate to attract the buyers who remain comfortable with the view. This effect tends to be strongest where the view is direct and close, and weaker or negligible where distance, trees, or another building screen the sightline.

It cuts both ways for pricing: some buyers specifically look for units with this kind of view precisely because it tends to enter the market at a more accessible price relative to comparable units elsewhere in the same development, and are entirely comfortable trading a smaller buyer pool at resale for a lower entry price today. Neither position is right or wrong; it depends entirely on your own view and your own resale horizon. How to evaluate objectively if you are not particular

If this concern does not affect you personally, it is still worth thinking about your own future exit. Check the exact distance and whether the sightline is direct or obstructed, check which floors and stacks in the block are actually affected versus which are screened by other buildings, and weigh any price advantage against how much it might narrow your own buyer pool when you eventually sell. Our guide to when not to buy in Singapore covers other situations worth this same kind of exit focused thinking.

For buyers who are particular

If this genuinely matters to you, ask your agent directly rather than assuming a listing photo tells the whole story; a photo taken from one angle may not show what a specific unit's actual windows face. View the unit at different times, including in the evening when void deck activity is most visible, and if it falls within a festival period such as Qing Ming or the Hungry Ghost month, viewing during that window will show you the site at its most active rather than its quietest. Buyers who remain interested despite a direct view often use curtains, blinds, or screening plants to reduce the sightline, and some consult a practitioner before committing, particularly for a higher quantum purchase.

Hospitals are a mixed case

Unlike cemeteries and funeral parlours, hospitals split opinion much more evenly among feng shui minded buyers. Some avoid proximity for the same yin energy reasoning, especially with a direct view of a mortuary or emergency entrance. Others do not weigh it at all, or actively value being close to a hospital for the convenience it offers ageing parents or family members with ongoing medical needs. Do not assume hospital proximity carries the same weight as the other categories in this guide; ask rather than assume, since views here genuinely differ more than they do for cemeteries.

Treat this as a personal and family decision, not a rule to enforce on anyone else. Beliefs and comfort levels around these sites vary enormously between individuals, families and generations, including within the same household. What one family member finds an obvious deal breaker, another may consider entirely irrelevant.

Checklist before you buy near a temple, hospital or cemetery

  • Check the exact distance from the unit to the site in question
  • Check whether the sightline is direct or obstructed by another building or greenery
  • Ask which specific floors and stacks in the block are actually affected
  • Ask your agent what the void deck below is used for, if relevant
  • View the unit in the evening as well as during the day
  • If it matters to you, view during a festival period such as Qing Ming or the Hungry Ghost month
  • Weigh any price advantage against your own future resale pool
  • Consider curtains, blinds or screening plants if you remain interested despite a direct view
  • Consult a practitioner before committing if this is a significant concern for you
  • Disclose the same consideration honestly to your own future buyer when you sell

Frequently asked questions

Is it bad feng shui to live near a temple?

Not automatically, and views differ by distance, orientation and the specific temple. Many practitioners distinguish between living near a temple in general, which is common across Singapore and rarely flagged on its own, and a unit with a direct, unobstructed view of a temple's rear, its incense burners, or its main altar, which is more commonly avoided. A temple viewed from a distance, or screened by other buildings, concerns far fewer buyers than one directly overlooked.

Does living near a cemetery affect resale value?

It can influence the size of the buyer pool for a specific unit rather than the town or estate as a whole. Agents commonly see fewer enquiries for units with a direct, close up view of a cemetery or columbarium compared with otherwise identical units without that view, which can mean a longer time on market or more room to negotiate. This is a qualitative pattern, not a fixed rule, and plenty of such units sell at fair prices to buyers who are simply not particular about it.

Do hospitals carry the same concern as cemeteries?

Not as consistently, and this is one of the more genuinely mixed cases. Some buyers avoid hospital proximity for similar yin energy reasons, particularly if a unit overlooks a mortuary or emergency entrance directly. Others do not mind at all, or actively prefer it for the convenience it offers elderly family members, making hospitals a less uniformly avoided category than cemeteries or funeral parlours among feng shui minded buyers.

Can curtains or plants offset a direct view of such a site?

Curtains, blinds, and screening plants can reduce a direct sightline and are a common, low cost step buyers who are particular but still interested in a unit will take. They address the practical discomfort of the view more reliably than they address any classical concern about the site itself, which some practitioners would say is not fully resolved by visual screening alone. Distance and obstruction from other buildings do more than any single remedy.

Should I mention this concern to my agent even if I am not particular?

Yes, worth raising even if you personally do not mind, since it affects your own future resale pool regardless of your own view today. An agent who knows this in advance can price and market the unit more accurately, and can prepare you for the kind of questions a future buyer is likely to raise when it is your turn to sell.
