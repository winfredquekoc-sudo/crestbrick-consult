Feng Shui Myths vs What Actually Matters in a Home

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick

Quick answer: Some feng shui rules describe good design in different language: light, layout and hygiene wrapped in tradition. Others, like your personal kua direction or avoiding the number 4, are cultural belief with no design mechanism behind them. Feng shui does not change how well a home is built, but an obvious taboo can shrink your future buyer pool, which is a real resale effect. Weigh the design overlaps seriously, hold the belief only layer as personal choice, and judge the whole unit rather than one rule in isolation.

Does feng shui really affect your home?

People ask this question in two different ways, and they deserve two different answers. Does feng shui affect how good a home actually is to live in? Partly, yes, because several of its oldest rules describe the same things an architect cares about: light, airflow, a sensible layout, and a clean line between wet and dry areas. Does feng shui affect your luck, wealth or health in a way outside of what good design already explains? That is a belief, held seriously by many Singaporean families, and this guide does not attempt to prove or disprove it.

This article sorts the common feng shui rules for a home into three groups. The first overlaps with good design, so it holds up under a plain, practical reading. The second is a personal or cultural belief with no design mechanism behind it, which this guide treats with respect rather than dismissal. The third is what actually moves resale value, which is a market question rather than a spiritual one. Keeping the three apart is what lets a buyer use feng shui well, instead of either ignoring it or reading too much into it.

Which feng shui rules overlap with good design?

A surprising number of classical feng shui rules point at the same conclusion a good architect would reach on different grounds. The table below sets six rules against the plain, physical reason they tend to make a home feel better, whatever you believe about qi.

Feng shui ruleWhat is really doing the work
A bright, open hall in front of the main doorNatural light and a tidy, uncluttered entry, which feel welcoming to you every day and show well to a future buyer.
The bed placed in a commanding position, able to see the doorYou are not startled by someone entering, and a solid wall behind the headboard settles the body for sleep.
No beam directly above the bedA large ceiling feature over your head reads as oppressive and can make a room feel smaller and less restful.
Main door or bedroom door not facing a toilet doorShared plumbing walls carry sound and sometimes smell. Distance and a closed door solve the practical problem as well as the traditional one.
A north south facing block rather than east westFewer hours of direct afternoon sun means a cooler home and a lower aircon bill through the year in Singapore.
A square or rectangular floor plan with no missing cornersFurniture fits properly and every square foot of the floor area earns its keep, which is exactly what a valuer and the next buyer respond to.

None of this requires believing in qi. A bright entrance, a calm bedroom and an efficient floor plan are good design in any language. Feng shui arrived at the same conclusions through a different tradition, long before terms like natural light and cross ventilation showed up in a property listing.

Which feng shui beliefs are personal or unprovable?

Not every feng shui rule has a design explanation, and that does not make it less real to the people who hold it. Your personal kua number and its associated good and bad directions, the location of the household wealth sector in a given year, and the choice to avoid a fourth floor or a unit number containing 4, are the clearest examples. None of these map onto light, noise, airflow or layout. They sit in a different part of feng shui altogether, closer to astrology or ancestral custom than to interior design.

This is not a reason to dismiss them. Many Singaporean and Chinese families treat these practices as part of a longstanding cultural and spiritual tradition, observed on its own terms rather than as a design checklist. If that tradition matters to you or your family, it is a legitimate factor in your decision, the same way a preference for a particular school zone is legitimate. What this guide will not do is dress up a belief as a proven fact, or claim it changes how the market prices a unit, because it generally does not.

For the personal layer, birth data, kua calculations, bazi and date selection, the right move is to engage a qualified feng shui master. That is a specialised skill, and it sits outside what a property analysis can tell you. Does feng shui affect resale value in Singapore?

There is no fixed feng shui discount or premium published anywhere, and no data set that prices it cleanly, because too many other factors move at the same time. What is real, and worth taking seriously, is demand. A meaningful share of Singapore buyers screen a unit on feng shui grounds before they even discuss price. A unit with an obvious structural taboo, a main door facing the lift head on, or a missing corner in the floor plan, speaks to fewer of them.

Fewer interested buyers can mean a longer time on the market or a softer final price when you eventually sell. That is a marketability effect working through ordinary supply and demand, not a mystical one. You are not buying luck when you avoid a taboo. You are protecting the size of your future buyer pool, on top of whatever daily comfort the same choice already gives you.

The line worth remembering: a structural taboo stays with the home and can narrow your buyer pool. A belief only preference, like a kua direction, stays with the person and rarely shows up in the sale price at all.

How should a practical buyer actually use feng shui?

Once you separate the three layers, feng shui becomes easy to use rather than a source of anxiety at every viewing. Start with what is structural and design linked: the main door facing, the floor plan shape, and anything that would need construction work to change. Weigh those seriously, because they affect daily living and they travel with the unit when you sell.

Treat cosmetic items lightly. A stove that faces the sink, a mirror opposite the bed, or a bed that is not yet in the commanding position are all changed in an afternoon, by you or by the next owner. They should not decide whether you buy.

Hold the belief only layer, your kua direction, the wealth sector, the number 4, as a personal filter you are free to apply, without expecting it to move the price or the physical quality of the home. A home can fail a belief only check and still be the right home, if the fundamentals, light, layout, location and condition, are strong.

What does this look like for a real buyer?

Daniel Ong is a Singapore citizen buying his second property, a resale condo unit in Queenstown, as his family outgrows their HDB flat. He shortlists a corner unit on a high floor with a north south block orientation and views it on a bright afternoon.

  • Light and layout. The unit sits corner, north south facing, with cross ventilation through the living and dining area. Every room is a usable rectangle, the kind of unit that shows well and holds its value.
  • Location. A short walk to the MRT, in a school zone Daniel wants for his children, in an estate with a deep pool of future buyers if he ever sells.
  • Main door and kitchen. Both pass. The door opens to a bright hall, and the stove sits at a right angle to the sink.
  • The one flag. The second bedroom door sits directly opposite the common bathroom door across a short passage, a classic minor taboo.

On the investor lens, Daniel weighs this properly rather than emotionally. The flagged item is fixable. He can keep both doors closed, or hang a curtain across the passage, and it stops being a daily concern. It is not a structural fault that would follow the unit to its next buyer, and it sits alongside a floor plan, light and location that are all genuinely strong. He proceeds with the purchase, and keeps the bathroom door shut out of habit rather than worry.

Frequently asked questions

Is feng shui just superstition?

Not exactly. Many of its rules for a home overlap with good design, like light, airflow and layout, which is why they hold up on a practical reading. Other parts, like personal kua directions, are cultural and spiritual belief that this guide treats with respect rather than proof.

Which feng shui rules actually affect daily living?

The ones with a design mechanism behind them: a bright main door, a bed in the commanding position, no beam overhead, and doors kept away from a toilet. These affect light, sleep, sound and hygiene regardless of what you believe about qi.

Does breaking a feng shui rule always hurt resale value?

No. A cosmetic item like a mirror facing the bed or a stove near the sink is fixable and rarely affects price. A structural item, like a main door facing the lift or a missing corner in the floor plan, can narrow your buyer pool and is worth weighing seriously.

Should I avoid a unit on the fourth floor or with the number 4?

That is a personal and cultural choice, not a design rule, so this guide does not tell you to avoid it. If it matters to you or your family, it is a legitimate factor in your decision. If it does not, a higher or lower floor number changes light, wind and noise far more than any number does.

What is the difference between a structural and a cosmetic feng shui issue?

A structural issue is built into the unit, like the main door facing or the shape of the floor plan, and you cannot change it without major renovation. A cosmetic issue, like furniture placement or a mirror, is changed in an afternoon by you or the next owner.

Do I need my kua number before choosing a home?

No. The design overlaps in this guide, light, layout, airflow and hygiene, apply to any buyer and need nothing about you personally. A kua number and other personal direction methods are a separate, more advanced layer, best handled by a qualified feng shui master.
