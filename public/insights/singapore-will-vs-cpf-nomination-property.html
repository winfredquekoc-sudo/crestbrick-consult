Singapore Will vs CPF Nomination: Which Controls Your Property When You Die?

By Winfred Quek · CEA R073319H · PropNex Realty

Quick answer: Your Will controls private property, bank accounts, investments, and personal assets. Your CPF nomination controls CPF monies (OA, SA, MA, RA) only — CPF cannot be devised by Will. For HDB held as joint tenancy, neither document applies — survivorship is automatic. You need both a Will AND a CPF nomination for complete estate coverage.

Why CPF Cannot Be in Your Will

The Central Provident Fund Act Section 25 explicitly states that CPF monies do not form part of a deceased's estate and cannot be disposed of by Will or intestacy. CPF balances are government-managed retirement savings — they have their own dedicated nomination system.

This means: even if your Will says "I leave everything to my children," your CPF balances do not go to your children under that Will. You must separately nominate them via CPF nomination.

What Happens to CPF Without a Nomination

If you die without a CPF nomination, the CPF Board transfers your entire CPF balance to the Public Trustee (a statutory body). The Public Trustee then distributes the money under the ISA formula — but charges an admin fee:

  • 1.25% on first $25,000
  • 0.75% on next $25,000
  • 0.50% on remainder above $50,000

For a $300K CPF balance: approximately $1,750 in Public Trustee fees, plus a process that takes 6+ months longer than a direct CPF nomination payout (which takes 2–3 months, no probate, no court).

The Will: What It Covers and Doesn't

Covered by Will:

  • Private property held as tenancy in common
  • Sole-owned private property
  • Bank accounts and cash deposits
  • Investments (shares, bonds, unit trusts)
  • Personal valuables, jewellery, art
  • Business interests
  • Overseas assets (subject to foreign laws)

NOT covered by Will:

  • CPF monies — requires CPF nomination
  • Property held as joint tenancy — survivorship applies
  • Life insurance with named beneficiaries (controlled by insurance nomination)
  • SRS (Supplementary Retirement Scheme) — controlled by SRS nomination

Marriage revokes your Will: Under the Wills Act, marriage automatically revokes any Will made before the marriage. If you get married and do nothing, your old Will is void — your estate is distributed by ISA. Make a new Will after every marriage. Divorce does not revoke a Will, but gifts to the ex-spouse are generally treated as void by operation of law.
