How commercial property loans differ from residential

By Winfred Quek · 9 minute read · Published 13 July 2026

By Winfred Quek, Associate Marketing Consultant · CEA R073319H · Crestbrick Pte Ltd (L31010886H) · Published 13 July 2026

Quick answer: A commercial property loan in Singapore, whether for a shop unit, office strata unit or industrial space, is not governed by the Total Debt Servicing Ratio or Mortgage Servicing Ratio that apply to residential loans. That sounds like more freedom, and in a narrow sense it is, but it comes packaged with three real constraints: a lower loan to value ceiling than the 75 percent available on a first residential home loan, no CPF usage at any stage of the purchase, and the likely presence of Goods and Services Tax on the transaction. If you have only ever bought residential property, the financing conversation for your first commercial purchase will feel less like a formula and more like a negotiation. This guide walks through what actually changes and how to plan your cash position around it.

Most buyers who come to commercial property for the first time are residential owners who have gotten comfortable with a set of rules: TDSR caps what you can borrow, CPF covers part of the down payment, and the bank's loan to value ceiling is a known number. None of those anchors carry over cleanly into commercial financing, and the buyers who get caught out are usually the ones who assumed the mechanics would simply scale up. They do not. Commercial financing runs on a different set of rules entirely, and understanding that early saves you from underestimating how much cash you actually need on hand.

Why commercial financing is a different game

The starting point is regulatory. TDSR and MSR exist specifically to manage household leverage against residential property, because residential property is where systemic risk to individual borrowers concentrates. Commercial and industrial property sit outside that framework. That does not mean commercial lending is unregulated. It means the specific ratio based ceiling that dictates how much of your income can go toward property debt does not apply here, and each bank instead applies its own underwriting discipline to a commercial application.

In practice this shifts the conversation from a fixed formula to a negotiation. A bank assessing a commercial loan will look at your income, your existing debt, your net worth, and critically, the property's own ability to generate rental income if that income is part of your repayment plan. Two buyers with identical income can receive different offers on the same unit, because the bank is pricing risk rather than applying a set ratio.

No TDSR or MSR: what that actually changes

For a strong income earner who is already near their TDSR ceiling on residential debt, the absence of TDSR on a commercial loan can open a door that would otherwise be shut. This is one reason some investors who have maxed out their residential borrowing capacity look at commercial or industrial property as the next avenue for leveraged property exposure, since a shophouse or an industrial unit purchase is not measured against the same 55 percent ceiling with its 4 percent stress test floor that governs a residential mortgage.

The trade off is that a bank without a regulatory ceiling to lean on will substitute its own judgement, and that judgement tends to be more conservative on quantum and more attentive to the specific property's fundamentals than a residential loan officer working within a known formula. Do not read "no TDSR" as "easier." Read it as "assessed differently, and often more subjectively."

Loan to value ceilings on commercial property

A first residential home loan from a bank can go up to 75 percent loan to value. Commercial property does not carry an equivalent standard ceiling set at that level. Each bank sets its own commercial LTV limits, and they vary by property type, by intended use, by lease tenure remaining, and by the borrower's overall relationship with the bank. What you should plan for, rather than a specific percentage, is the near certainty that you will need to fund a meaningfully larger portion of the purchase price yourself than you would on a residential purchase.

Budget the cash gap early. The single most common planning mistake first time commercial buyers make is assuming their residential down payment experience will translate directly. It will not. Get an indicative LTV from your bank before you commit to a unit, not after, because the gap between what you expected to borrow and what the bank will actually lend can be the difference between a deal that closes and one that does not.

CPF: the tool you lose

CPF Ordinary Account savings can only be used for residential property purchases under the CPF housing schemes. There is no equivalent mechanism for commercial or industrial property. Every dollar of the down payment, every dollar of stamp duty, and every monthly instalment on a commercial loan has to come from cash or from other financing, not from your CPF balance.

For a buyer used to leaning on CPF as a meaningful part of their residential purchasing power, this is often the single biggest mental adjustment. It means your actual liquid cash position, not your CPF balance, is what determines whether a commercial purchase is within reach, and it means the opportunity cost of that cash, what else it could be earning at the CPF Ordinary Account's 2.5 percent or elsewhere, is part of the real cost of the purchase.

GST and how it enters the financing conversation

Goods and Services Tax generally applies to sales of non residential property, in contrast to residential property sales which are typically exempt from GST. Whether GST is actually charged on your purchase, and whether you as a buyer can register for GST and claim input tax credits, depends on how you are structured as a buyer, whether you intend to use the property for a taxable business purpose, and how the seller is registered. This is not a detail to leave until the eleventh hour. It affects your total funding requirement and, depending on structure, may itself need to be financed or paid upfront in cash before any GST input claim is processed. Get your accountant involved before you sign anything, not after.

How banks assess a commercial loan instead

Without TDSR and MSR as the primary lens, banks lean more heavily on a handful of other factors: the strength and stability of your income, whether the property has an existing tenant with a lease in place, the remaining lease tenure on the property itself, the sector and outlook for that type of commercial or industrial space, and your track record if you are an existing borrower with the bank. A vacant unit with no income history is a materially harder loan to underwrite than a tenanted unit with a running lease, because the bank can see the cash flow rather than take your word for the rental projection.

If you are buying with the intention of leasing it out, walk into the financing conversation with a realistic rental case, not an optimistic one. Banks discount projected rental income when assessing serviceability, and an inflated number in your own plan will only mean you discover the real number, and the real cash gap, later rather than earlier.

Getting the sequencing right

  1. Get an indicative LTV and rate before you shop for units. Commercial financing terms vary enough between banks and property types that walking in with a firm budget only after falling for a specific unit is how buyers overcommit.
  2. Size your cash position honestly, without CPF. Include the down payment gap from the lower LTV, stamp duty, legal fees, and any GST exposure, all funded from cash or non CPF financing.
  3. Bring a real rental case, not a hopeful one. If income from the property is part of how you plan to service the loan, underwrite it the way the bank will, conservatively.
  4. Loop in your accountant on GST before you commit. Structure decisions made after signing an Option to Purchase are far more expensive to unwind than ones made before.

None of this makes commercial property a bad idea. For the right buyer, with the right cash position and a clear eyed rental case, it is a legitimate way to diversify beyond a residential portfolio that may already be constrained by ABSD or TDSR. It simply is not residential financing with a different signboard, and treating it that way is the fastest way to misjudge how much cash the purchase actually requires.

Frequently asked questions

Does TDSR apply to commercial property loans in Singapore?

No. The Total Debt Servicing Ratio framework applies to loans secured against residential property. A loan to buy a shop unit, office strata unit or industrial unit is not subject to the 55 percent TDSR ceiling or the Mortgage Servicing Ratio that applies to HDB flats and executive condominiums. Your bank will still assess your income and existing debts, but it is not bound by the same regulatory formula.

What loan to value can I get on commercial property in Singapore?

Commercial property loans typically carry a lower loan to value ceiling than the 75 percent available on a first residential home loan. Individual banks set their own commercial LTV limits and they vary by property type, use, and the borrower's profile, so the exact ceiling is a bank by bank commercial decision rather than a fixed regulatory number. Expect to fund a larger slice of the purchase price in cash than you would on a residential purchase.

Can I use CPF for a commercial property purchase?

No. CPF savings can only be used for residential property under the CPF housing schemes. Commercial and industrial property purchases, including the down payment, stamp duty and monthly instalments, must be funded entirely from cash or other financing, not CPF Ordinary Account funds.

Is GST payable on commercial property purchases in Singapore?

Goods and Services Tax generally applies to the sale of non residential property, whereas residential property sales are typically exempt. Whether GST is charged, and whether you can register for GST and claim input tax, depends on your buyer structure and how the property will be used, so this needs to be worked out with your accountant or conveyancing lawyer before you commit to a purchase price.

Is financing commercial property harder to get than residential?

It is different rather than simply harder. Because TDSR and MSR do not apply, a bank has more discretion in how it assesses you, which can work for or against a buyer depending on the strength of the underlying income and the property's own rental potential. What is consistently true is that you need more cash upfront because of the lower LTV ceiling and the unavailability of CPF, and you should expect the bank to look closely at the property's rental income if that income is part of your repayment story.
