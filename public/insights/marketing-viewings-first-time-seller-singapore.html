Part 4 of this series covered getting the unit itself ready. This part covers getting buyers through the door, where they actually come from, what earns a click, how to host a viewing well, and how to read the feedback that comes back afterward.

Where buyers for your property actually come from

For most sellers, the major property portals remain the primary discovery channel, since this is where the largest pool of active buyers searches first. Agent networks add a second, meaningful channel, since an experienced agent's own contact base often includes buyers already searching in your specific area or price range. A significant share of transactions also happen through co-broke arrangements, where an agent representing a buyer brings that buyer to your listing in exchange for a share of commission, which widens your effective buyer pool well beyond your own listing's direct reach. Personal networks and word of mouth can produce a buyer too, but they are a supplement to the portals and agent networks, not a replacement for them.

What makes a listing worth clicking

A buyer scrolling a portal decides within seconds whether a listing is worth opening. Three things earn that click and, more importantly, earn the right kind of click from a genuinely interested buyer.

Clear, well lit photographs

Taken after the preparation covered in part 4 of this series, shot in good light, from angles that actually show the space rather than a single flattering corner.

An accurate floor plan

Buyers use the floor plan to judge whether the layout works for their needs before they ever request a viewing, which filters in serious buyers and filters out ones for whom the layout was never going to work anyway.

Honest, specific copy

A description that names the actual features, renovation age, orientation, storage, rather than generic phrases repeated across every listing in the area. Specific, accurate copy also protects you, since a property agent's marketing is expected to be accurate and not misleading under CEA's own conduct rules.

Selling yourself vs using an agent

Some first time sellers consider marketing and negotiating the sale themselves rather than engaging an agent. This is a genuine option, and our guide to selling yourself versus using an agent covers the trade offs in depth. The short version is that doing it yourself can save on commission, but it shifts the marketing, screening, negotiation and paperwork entirely onto you, along with the risk of a costly mistake in an area you have not navigated before. Whichever route you choose, deciding on exclusive or open representation if you do engage an agent is worth understanding upfront, covered in our exclusive versus open listing guide.

Scheduling and hosting viewings well

Before the viewing: Confirm the appointment, ventilate and light the unit as covered in part 4, and clear the space of anything not meant to be seen.
During the viewing: Give buyers room to look and talk among themselves rather than narrating every feature. Be ready to answer direct questions about age of fittings, renovation history and any known issues honestly.
After the viewing: Ask for feedback promptly, while the visit is still fresh, rather than weeks later when the buyer has moved on to other listings.

Reading buyer feedback properly

A single comment from a single buyer is one data point, not a verdict. Some buyers simply were never a fit for your unit, regardless of price or presentation. What deserves your attention is a pattern, several unrelated viewers independently raising the same concern, whether that is the price, a specific room, or the general condition. A repeated theme across multiple viewings is the closest thing to real market feedback you will get before an offer arrives, and it is worth acting on.

When no viewings means the price is the message

If your listing is genuinely well presented, with the photos, floor plan and copy covered above, and still generates very few viewing requests, resist the urge to keep tweaking photos or copy. Our guides on what to do if your property won't sell and selling fast in a slow market both point to the same underlying diagnosis in most cases: the price sits outside the range buyers are willing to click into, which is a pricing problem, covered in part 3 of this series, not a marketing one.

Do not confuse activity with progress. Refreshing photos, rewriting the description, or switching between portals repeatedly can feel like doing something, but if viewing volume stays low after a genuine, well executed launch, the honest next step is almost always a price review, not another round of marketing changes.

Winfred's Take

I tell every first time seller the same thing about viewings, the market is talking to you constantly, you just have to listen for the pattern instead of the individual comment. One buyer who did not like the layout tells you nothing. Five buyers who all mention the same thing tell you everything. The sellers who struggle are usually the ones treating every single piece of feedback as either gospel or noise, instead of watching for the signal that only shows up once several strangers say the same thing independently.

Frequently asked questions

Where do most genuine buyers for my property actually come from?

The large property portals remain the primary discovery channel for most buyers, supplemented by agent networks who bring pre qualified buyers already searching in your area, and co-broke arrangements where another agent represents a buyer for your listing. A small number of buyers come through personal networks or direct outreach, but the portals and agent networks carry the bulk of genuine, financeable interest for most listings.

Should I sell my property myself or use an agent?

It depends on your time, your comfort negotiating directly with buyers and other agents, and how much of the marketing, screening and paperwork you want to handle yourself. Selling yourself can save on commission but shifts all of that work, and the associated risk of mistakes, onto you. Compare the trade offs honestly against your own situation rather than assuming one path is automatically better.

What actually makes a listing worth clicking on a portal?

Clear, well lit photographs taken after proper preparation, an accurate floor plan, and honest, specific copy that describes the unit rather than generic phrases repeated across every listing. Buyers scan dozens of listings quickly, so a listing that is genuinely informative and visually clean earns more clicks, and more of the right clicks, than one that relies on vague superlatives.

How should I read feedback after a viewing?

Look for patterns across multiple viewings rather than reacting to any single comment. If several buyers independently mention the same concern, price, a specific room, the condition of something, treat that as real signal. A single buyer's opinion is just one data point, but a repeated theme across unrelated viewers is the market telling you something worth acting on.

What does it mean if my listing is getting no viewings at all?

If your listing is well presented and marketed but is not generating viewing requests, the price is very likely the actual message, not the photos or the description. Buyers who might otherwise be interested are filtering the listing out before they even open it. This is the point to revisit your pricing, covered in part 3 of this series, rather than making further changes to photos or copy.
